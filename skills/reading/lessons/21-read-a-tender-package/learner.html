Read a tender package

Find your way through the drawings, specifications and addenda in a bid package, and use the order of precedence to decide which document wins when they disagree.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 1-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Identify the task that requires you to read; Identify the information; Make connections between different parts of the document(s); Evaluate the document(s)

Why this matters on the job

Before a company bids on a job, an estimator reads the whole tender package and prices exactly what it asks for. On site, the crew builds from the same documents. Sometimes a drawing says one thing and the specification says another. If you guess, you may build it wrong, tear it out and do it again on your own time. A tender package tells you which document wins. This lesson builds on the drawing lessons (Reading lessons 11 to 18).

Key ideas

1. What is in a tender package. A tender (or bid) package is the set of documents a contractor uses to price a job. It usually has: - Instructions to bidders: the rules for the bid, such as the closing date and how to ask questions. - A bid form: where the contractor writes the price and lists the addenda it received. - The contract and its conditions. - Drawings. - Specifications ("specs"). - Addenda: changes issued before the bid closes.

2. Drawings and specs do different jobs. - Drawings show what and where: the location, shape and size of the work. - Specifications are written requirements: which materials and products, what quality, and how the work is done.

3. Specs are filed by number. Most specs in Canada are organized with MasterFormat numbers: three pairs of digits. The first pair is the division. For example, the London Square spec from Reading lesson 18 lists: - Division 01: General Requirements (rules for every trade on the job) - 05 50 00: Metal Fabrications - 06 10 00: Rough Carpentry - 09 91 00: Painting

To find a rule, start at the spec's table of contents, find the section, then read it.

4. Addenda change the package. An addendum is a formal written change issued during bidding. Once issued, it becomes part of the bid documents, and the price must include it. A later addendum replaces anything earlier that it changes. List every addendum on the bid form. A phone call or a chat on site does not change the documents. Only a written addendum does.

5. Order of precedence. When two documents disagree, the package says which one wins. This list is called the order of precedence. Standard Canadian contracts, such as CCDC 2 (2020), set an order like this. Common rules: - Specifications win over drawings. - On drawings of the same date, the larger-scale drawing wins (a 1:10 detail beats a 1:50 plan). - A written dimension wins over a dimension you measure with a scale rule. - A later document wins over an earlier one of the same type.

Always read the order of precedence in your package. Do not assume.

6. When the rules do not settle it, ask. If two documents disagree and the order of precedence does not decide it, ask the design team in writing before the question deadline: see Communication lesson 12.

Worked example

Question: What colour is the finish paint on the guard?

  1. Go to the spec table of contents (Document C). Paint is in section 09 91 00 Painting.
  2. Section 09 91 00 says: finish colour "Charcoal".
  3. Check the addenda (Document D). Addendum 2, dated March 3, says: "09 91 00 Painting. Change finish colour from Charcoal to Slate Blue."
  4. A later document wins over an earlier one (Document A, item 5d). Slate Blue.

Practice documents

All documents below are for a fictional job.

Document A: Instructions to bidders (excerpt)

Tool library fit-out, Unit 4 (fictional building) Issued by: Alder & Moss Architects (fictional company), for the owner, the Riverside Tool Library (fictional) Issued for tender: February 10

  1. Bids close at 2:00 p.m. on March 12. Late bids are not opened.
  2. Send all questions in writing to the architect by March 5. No questions are answered after that date.
  3. Any change to the bid documents is made only by a written addendum from the architect. No other answer, written or spoken, changes the documents.
  4. List every addendum you received on the bid form.
  5. If the documents disagree, this order applies: a) Addenda, the latest first b) Specifications c) Drawings. On drawings of the same date, the larger-scale drawing governs. d) A later-dated document governs over an earlier one of the same type. e) Written dimensions govern. Do not scale the drawings.

Document B: Drawing list (all dated February 10)

Sheet Title Scale
A-101 Floor plan 1:50
A-301 Details 1:10
A-401 Door schedule none

Document C: Specifications (excerpts)

Table of contents Division 01 · 01 11 00 Summary of Work Division 05 · 05 50 00 Metal Fabrications Division 06 · 06 10 00 Rough Carpentry Division 09 · 09 91 00 Painting

05 50 00 Metal Fabrications. Steel guard at mezzanine: steel pipe rails, shop primed with one coat of grey primer by the fabricator. 06 10 00 Rough Carpentry. Plywood backing for wall-mounted tool racks: 19 mm plywood, fastened to every stud. 09 91 00 Painting. Finish colour: Charcoal. Painter primes all bare steel on site before finish coats.

Document D: Addenda

Addendum 1 · February 24 A-401 Door schedule. Door D2: change width from 900 mm to 1000 mm.

Addendum 2 · March 3 09 91 00 Painting. Change finish colour from Charcoal to Slate Blue.

Document E: What the drawings show

  • A-101 (1:50): The workbench wall has a written dimension of 3000 mm. Measured with a scale rule on the printed sheet, the wall is 62 mm long.
  • A-101 (1:50): A note says "Tool counter 600 mm deep."
  • A-301 Detail 2 (1:10): The section through the tool counter shows the counter 650 mm deep.
  • A-301 Detail 4 (1:10): A note says "12 mm plywood backing behind tool racks."

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Use Document A. a) When do bids close? b) What is the last day to send a question? c) Who can change the bid documents, and how?

Task 2 (level 1) Use the table of contents in Document C. Which spec section would you open to find: a) the plywood for the tool racks? b) the steel guard? c) the paint?

Task 3 (level 2) Would you look on a drawing or in the specifications first for each of these? Use Key idea 2. a) where the tool counter goes in the room b) the thickness of the plywood backing c) how many coats of primer the steel gets d) the shape of the guard at the top of the stair e) the paint colour

Task 4 (level 2) The workbench wall on A-101 has a written dimension of 3000 mm. Someone measures the printed sheet with a scale rule and gets 62 mm at 1:50. a) What length does 62 mm at 1:50 stand for? b) Which length do you build to? Name the rule in Document A.

Task 5 (level 2) A-101 says the tool counter is 600 mm deep. Detail 2 on A-301 shows it 650 mm deep. Both sheets are dated February 10. Which depth do you price and build? Why?

Task 6 (level 2) Detail 4 on A-301 says 12 mm plywood backing. Spec section 06 10 00 says 19 mm. Which wins? Why?

Task 7 (level 3) a) How wide is door D2 now? Which document tells you? b) An estimator fills in the bid form and lists only Addendum 1. What is wrong, and what could it cost the company? c) On March 4, a site visitor tells the estimator, "Don't worry about the paint, the owner said grey is fine." Does this change the documents? Why or why not?

Task 8 (level 3) Section 05 50 00 says the fabricator shop primes the guard. Section 09 91 00 says the painter primes all bare steel on site. a) Do these instructions necessarily conflict? What handover detail about bare or damaged areas would you clarify before pricing? b) Today is March 2. Write the question you would send to the architect.

Check yourself

  1. What do drawings show, and what do specifications say?
  2. What is an addendum?
  3. A 1:10 detail and a 1:50 plan of the same date disagree. Which wins in most packages?
  4. Why should you not scale a drawing when a written dimension is given?
  5. Does a phone call from the architect change the bid documents?
