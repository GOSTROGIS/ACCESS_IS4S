Topographic maps and site plans

Read the scale, contour lines, spot elevations, grid and legend on a topographic map and a site plan, and use them to find heights, distances, slope and which way water will run.

Session 26 of 26 in the Reading lessons · 90 minutes

Before the session: project Figures 1 and 2, or print them. They are drawn on the learner page, so they print at any size. If you can, bring one real paper topographic map (any NRCan 1:50 000 sheet) and a ruler, so learners can measure on a sheet printed at true scale and read a real margin and legend. Learners need a calculator for Tasks 7, 10 and 11. This lesson builds on Reading lesson 14 (Drawing scales) and lesson 17 (grids, north arrows and legends). Digital lesson 9 covers online maps and BC 1 Call.

Opening (5 min)

Welcome learners. Ask: "If it rains hard on a lot where we just dug the footings, where does the water go?" Take answers. Then ask: "How would you know before you dig?" Show Figure 2 and point at the corner numbers. Tell learners that the drawings already answer the question if you know how to read the numbers and the dashed lines. Then show Figure 1 and say the same ideas work over kilometres, for a road into a camp job or a creek crossing.

This is session 26 of 26 in the Reading lessons. It runs 90 minutes.

Objective (3 min)

By the end of the session, learners will be able to:

  • find the scale, contour interval, spot elevations, grid numbers and legend on a map and a site plan (Identify the information)
  • connect contour lines, spot elevations and the creek to work out which way the ground slopes (Make connections between different parts of the document(s))
  • give a grid reference, measure a distance with a scale, and work out a fall and a percent grade (Understand and apply the information)
  • judge whether a map or drawing is current enough, and when not to measure it (Evaluate the document(s))

Pre-assessment (5 min)

  1. "On a map, what does 1:50 000 mean?"
  2. Show Figure 2 for 15 seconds. "Which corner of this lot is highest?"

Post-assessment (9 min)

  1. "On a 1:50 000 map, two points are 5 cm apart. How far apart are they on the ground?" (5 × 50 000 = 250 000 cm = 2.5 km.)
  2. "On a site plan, two contour lines are labelled 100.0 and 100.5. Which one is uphill?" (100.5.)
  3. "Give the grid reference of the 411 spot elevation in Figure 1." (545627: easting 54 plus 5 tenths, northing 62 plus 7 tenths.)

Summary (6 min)

During this session we covered:

  • map scales such as 1:50 000 and 1:250 000, and turning a map distance into a ground distance and back
  • contour lines, the contour interval, index contours, close and far spacing, and the V that points upstream
  • spot elevations and benchmarks
  • north on a topographic map and the north arrow on a site plan
  • six-figure grid references: easting first, then northing
  • falls and percent grade on a site plan
  • checking a map's date, and using written dimensions instead of measuring

Total time: 5 + 3 + 5 + 62 + 9 + 6 = 90 minutes.

Notes for the instructor

  • The figures are not to true scale on a screen. Figure 1 is labelled 1:50 000 and Figure 2 is labelled 1:200, but a screen or a printer changes their size. That is why every task on the figures uses the grid squares, the scale bar or the written dimensions, and why Task 7 b and c ask about a real paper map. This is the same lesson as Reading lesson 14: use the scale bar or the written dimension.
  • Grid squares. Figure 1 states its own grid size (1 km). On a real map, read the grid size and the grid numbers from the margin of that map.
  • Exact positions. In the drawing, the cabin is at easting 52.3 and northing 62.7, the bridge at 53.4 and 61.3, and the 411 spot elevation at 54.5 and 62.7. Learners estimating tenths by eye may be one tenth off. Accept that if their method is right.
  • Indigenous place knowledge. Learners may know the land in their own territories better than any map. Invite them to compare a topographic map of a place they know with what they know of it, if they want to. Do not supply cultural or place-name content yourself.

  • The most frequently used Canadian topographic map is at 1:50 000, where 2 cm on the map represents 1 km on the ground. A 1:250 000 NTS map covers the same area as sixteen 1:50 000 maps. Contour lines connect points of equal elevation above mean sea level. Lines close together mean steeper slopes and lines far apart mean gentler or flat ground. Contour elevation numbers read (point) uphill. A spot elevation is a point where the height above mean sea level is noted, usually by a dot and a value. Topographic maps show a UTM grid in metres, with eastings read along the north and south edges and northings along the east and west edges. Find the easting first, then the northing ("In the house, then up the stairs"), using the grid line left of and below the feature and estimating tenths of a square. North is always at the top of a topographic map. Review the map data and publication date in the bottom left corner. Natural Resources Canada, "Topographic Maps: The basics" (2014), https://natural-resources.canada.ca/sites/nrcan/files/earthsciences/pdf/topo101/pdf/mapping_basics_e.pdf, checked 2026-11-08. This guide is Crown copyright, so the lesson links to it and does not reproduce it.
  • On a 1:50 000 map, 1 cm = ½ km, and on a 1:250 000 map, 1 cm = 2½ km. Use the scale bar at the bottom of the map. NRCan topographic maps are in two standard scales, 1:50 000 and 1:250 000. Natural Resources Canada, "Topographic Maps - Tips and Hints", https://natural-resources.canada.ca/maps-tools-publications/maps/topographic-maps/topographic-maps-tips-hints, and "Topographic maps", https://natural-resources.canada.ca/maps-tools-publications/maps/topographic-maps, both checked 2026-11-08.
  • Heavier index contours are labelled with their elevation, the contour interval is the elevation difference between adjacent contours, and it is noted in the map margin. US Geological Survey, "US Topo Cartographic Specifications - Map Symbol Guide", https://www.usgs.gov/ngp-standards-and-specifications/us-topo-map-symbol-guide, checked 2026-11-08. Index contours are every fourth or fifth contour, depending on the interval. US Geological Survey, "Topographic Maps for Development", https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/70039399/report.pdf, checked 2026-11-08.
  • Contours are V-shaped in stream beds and narrow valleys, and the point of the V always points uphill or upstream. National Wildfire Coordinating Group, "S-390 Pre-Course Study Material: Topography", https://training.nwcg.gov/dl/s390/s-390-topography.pdf, checked 2026-11-08.
