# Topographic maps and site plans

## Why this matters on the job

Before a crew digs, sets forms or plans a drain, someone has to know how the ground lies. Which corner of the lot is high? Where will rain water run? How steep is the road into a remote camp job? A **topographic map** shows the land around a job over many kilometres. A **site plan** shows one lot, close up. Both use the same ideas: a scale, contour lines, spot elevations, a north direction and a legend. This lesson follows [Reading lesson 14, Drawing scales](../14-drawing-scales/learner.html), and [Reading lesson 17, Symbols, legends, grids and schedules](../17-symbols-legends-grids-and-schedules/learner.html).

## Key ideas

**1. Scale at map sizes.** A map scale compares a distance on the map with the real distance on the ground. The most-used Canadian topographic map is **1:50 000**. On it, **2 cm on the map is 1 km on the ground**, so 1 cm is 500 m (half a kilometre). Natural Resources Canada (NRCan) also makes **1:250 000** maps, where 1 cm is 2.5 km. One 1:250 000 sheet covers the same area as sixteen 1:50 000 sheets.

- Real distance = map distance × the scale number. Then change the units: 100 cm = 1 m, and 1000 m = 1 km.
- Map distance = real distance ÷ the scale number.
- A **scale bar** is printed at the bottom of the map. Use it on a screen or a copy, because a copy may not be printed at true size.

**2. Contour lines.** A contour line joins points of the **same elevation above mean sea level**.
- The **contour interval** is the difference in elevation between two lines next to each other. It is printed in the margin of the map.
- On many maps, every fourth or fifth line is a heavier **index contour** with its elevation written on it.
- Lines **close together** mean a **steep** slope. Lines **far apart** mean a gentle slope. Very far apart means flat ground.
- Contour numbers are printed so that they read uphill.
- Where a contour line crosses a creek, it bends into a **V that points upstream**.
- Water runs downhill, from a higher line toward a lower line, straight across the lines.

**3. Spot elevations.** A spot elevation is a point with its height written beside it, usually a dot and a number. Topographic maps show them at places like hilltops, road crossings and lakes. Site plans show them at lot corners and other key points.

**4. Which way is north.** On an NRCan topographic map, north is always at the top. On a site plan, look for the north arrow. Do not assume.

**5. The grid and grid references.** A topographic map has a square grid. On NRCan maps it is the UTM grid, measured in metres. Eastings are written along the top and bottom edges. Northings are written along the sides. To give a six-figure grid reference:
1. **Easting first.** Read the number of the grid line to the left of the feature. Then estimate how many tenths of a square the feature is to the east of it.
2. **Then northing.** Read the number of the grid line below the feature. Then estimate how many tenths of a square it is to the north.

NRCan's memory trick: "In the house, then up the stairs." Go along first, then up.

**6. The legend and the margin.** The legend tells you what each symbol means. The margin also gives the scale, the contour interval, and the year and edition of the map. NRCan says to check the map data and publication date in the bottom left corner. Roads, buildings and clearings made after that date may not be on the map.

A map never shows what is buried. Before any digging, follow the BC 1 Call steps in [Digital lesson 9](../../../digital/lessons/09-maps-and-site-location/learner.html).

### Worked example

**Question:** Use Figure 1. What is the six-figure grid reference of the cabin, and is the cabin higher or lower than 400 m?

1. **Easting.** The grid line to the left of the cabin is **52**. The cabin is about **3 tenths** of a square east of it. Easting **523**.
2. **Northing.** The grid line below the cabin is **62**. The cabin is about **7 tenths** of a square north of it. Northing **627**.
3. **Grid reference: 523627.**
4. **Height.** The heavy line labelled **400** is south of the cabin. The ground rises to the north (the numbers get bigger going north, and the V shapes point north, upstream). So the cabin is **higher than 400 m**.
