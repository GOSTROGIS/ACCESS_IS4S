Sections, details and callouts

Read cutting-plane lines and section views, name common material hatch patterns, and follow section marks and detail callouts from one sheet to another.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)

  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Identify the information; Make connections between different parts of the document(s); Understand and apply the information; Evaluate the document(s)

Why this matters on the job

Plans and elevations show the outside of things. To see how a wall, a footing or a pipe fitting is built inside, drawings cut through it and show the cut face. These cut views are called sections. Small, tricky areas, such as where a wall sits on a foundation, are drawn bigger as details. Small symbols on the drawings, called callouts, tell you which sheet to turn to. A drawing set can have hundreds of sheets. If you can follow a callout, you can find the one detail that answers your question. This lesson follows Reading lesson 13 (lines) and Reading lesson 14 (scales).

Key ideas

1. Cutting plane and section view. A cutting-plane line (thick, with a long dash and two short dashes) shows where the object is cut. The arrows at its ends show which way you look at the cut. The letters at the ends, such as A-A, name the section. The section view is labelled with the same letters, such as SECTION A-A.

2. Section lines (hatching). In a section view, the surfaces the cut passes through are filled with thin lines or a pattern. Holes and empty spaces are left blank. Edges you see beyond the cut are drawn as object lines. Hidden lines are usually not needed.

3. Kinds of section.

  • Full section: the cut goes all the way through the object.
  • Half section: one quarter of the object is cut away. Half the view shows the outside and half shows the inside. It is used for round, matching parts such as bushings and flanges.
  • Broken-out section: only a small area is cut open, bounded by a thick, wavy break line. It shows one feature, such as a drilled hole, without cutting the whole part.

4. Material hatch patterns. On building drawings, the pattern tells you the material. Common ones are concrete (dots and small triangles), earth, gravel, brick (45° lines), concrete block, wood framing cut across the grain (a box with an X), finish wood, steel, batt insulation (a looping line) and rigid insulation (a crosshatch). Patterns are not the same in every office. Always check the legend of the drawing set.

5. Section marks. On a plan, a section is shown by a cut line with a bubble at the end. The bubble is split by a line. The top number is the section number. The bottom is the sheet it is drawn on. 1 over A-301 means section 1 on sheet A-301. A solid triangle on the bubble points the way you look.

6. Detail callouts. A circle or box drawn around an area, with a bubble such as 3 over A-501, means that area is drawn bigger as detail 3 on sheet A-501. Elevation markers use the same kind of bubble, with a triangle pointing at the wall that is shown.

7. Drawing titles close the loop. Under each section or detail is a title with the same bubble, its name and its scale. When you arrive at sheet A-501, look for the title bubble 3 over A-501. Details are drawn at a bigger scale (such as 1:5 or 3" = 1'-0") so small parts can be read.

8. Sheet numbers. The letter at the front of a sheet number often tells you the discipline, such as A for architectural or S for structural. The number tells you where the sheet sits in the set. Real drawing sets are covered in Reading lesson 16.

Worked example 1: read a section view

Question: In Figure 1, the bushing flange is 100 mm across, the hub is 60 mm across, and the bore is 30 mm across. How thick is the hub wall, and why are there blank gaps in the flange in SECTION A-A?

  1. The cutting-plane line goes straight through the centre, so the section shows the full width of each part.
  2. Hub wall thickness = (hub diameter − bore) ÷ 2 = (60 − 30) ÷ 2 = 30 ÷ 2 = 15 mm. Brackets first (BEDMAS).
  3. In the top view, the cutting-plane line passes through the two small bolt holes. A hole has no steel to cut, so in the section it is left blank. The lines across the top and bottom of each gap are the far side of the hole.

Answer: the hub wall is 15 mm thick. The gaps are the two bolt holes, cut through their centres.

Worked example 2: follow the callouts

Question: A carpenter on the floor plan (A-101) needs to know how the sill plate sits on the foundation wall. Where does she look?

  1. On A-101, she finds the section mark on the outside wall. The bubble reads 1 over A-301.
  2. She turns to sheet A-301 and finds the title bubble 1 over A-301: WALL SECTION, 1:20 (Figure 3).
  3. On the wall section, the sill area is circled. The bubble reads 3 over A-501.
  4. She turns to sheet A-501 and finds the title bubble 3 over A-501: SILL DETAIL, 1:5.

Answer: detail 3 on sheet A-501, reached from A-101 through section 1 on A-301.

Worked example 3: how much bigger is the detail?

Question: The wall section is at 1:20 and the sill detail is at 1:5. The sill plate is 38 mm thick. How thick is it drawn on each sheet, and how many times bigger is the detail?

  1. At 1:20: 38 ÷ 20 = 1.9 mm on paper.
  2. At 1:5: 38 ÷ 5 = 7.6 mm on paper.
  3. 20 ÷ 5 = 4. The detail is drawn 4 times bigger. Check: 1.9 × 4 = 7.6.

Answer: 1.9 mm on the wall section, 7.6 mm on the detail. The detail is 4 times bigger.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Use Figure 1. a) What do the arrows on cutting-plane line A-A tell you? b) What is the name of the view drawn from that cut? c) What do the thin diagonal lines in that view show?

Task 2 (level 1) Use Figure 4. Say in your own words what each bubble means. a) 1 over A-301 on a section mark b) 3 over A-501 on a detail callout c) 2 over A-201 on an elevation marker

Task 3 (level 1) Use Figure 2. Which material does each pattern stand for? a) A box with a diagonal cross b) Dots and small triangles c) A looping line d) A crosshatch

Task 4 (level 2) Use Figure 1. a) What does the half section show that the full section does not? b) Why is a half section a good choice for this bushing? c) Why did the drafter use a broken-out section for the shaft instead of a full section?

Task 5 (level 2) Use Figures 2 and 3. Name the material at each letter A to F. Say which pattern told you.

Task 6 (level 2) Use Figure 1 and Worked example 1. The flange is 100 mm across and the hub is 60 mm across. a) How far does the flange stick out past the hub on each side? b) The bolt holes are Ø10, with centres 40 mm from the centre of the bushing. How far from the centre is the inner edge of each hole? The outer edge?

Task 7 (level 2) Use Figure 5. a) A framer on A-101 needs to see how the whole outside wall is built, from footing to wall. Which sheet and which view? b) A plumber needs to see exactly where the sill plate sits on the concrete. Which sheet and which detail? c) What scale is each of those views drawn at?

Task 8 (level 2) A pipe sleeve through the foundation wall is 100 mm across. How big is it drawn: a) on the 1:20 wall section? b) on a 1:5 detail? c) How many times bigger is the 1:5 drawing?

Task 9 (level 3) On a real job, the wall section on A-301 has a callout bubble reading 3 over A-501. You go to sheet A-501. It has details 1, 2 and 4, but no detail 3. What could have happened? What do you do before you build that part?

Task 10 (level 3) A small drawing set has no material legend. A wall in section is filled with 45° lines. A co-worker says, "That's brick." Use Key idea 4 and Figure 2 to explain why you cannot be sure. List two places in the drawing set where you could look to find out, and say what you do if you still cannot tell.

Task 11 (level 3) You are the lead hand. Write the steps you would give a new apprentice to find the detail for the sill in Figure 5, starting from the floor plan. Use the words section mark, bubble, sheet number and title.

  5. Why are details drawn at a bigger scale than plans?
