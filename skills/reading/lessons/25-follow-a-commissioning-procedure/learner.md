# Follow a commissioning procedure

## Why this matters on the job

**Commissioning** means proving that a new machine is installed right and runs safely before it is handed over to the people who will use it every day. On a new conveyor, pump or fan, the electrician, the millwright and the supervisor each have steps to do, in a set order. The steps are written down, because starting a machine that is wired wrong, misaligned or unguarded can wreck the machine or hurt someone. Millwrights and electricians read procedures like this one on every install. This lesson uses a fictional procedure. On a real job you follow your employer's written procedure, the manufacturer's manual and your supervisor.

This lesson builds on [Reading lesson 3, Follow written instructions](../03-follow-written-instructions/learner.html) and [Reading lesson 8, Use an equipment manual](../08-use-an-equipment-manual/learner.html).

## Key ideas

**1. Read the whole procedure first.** A procedure for a machine is long and split into sections. A warning in Section 4 can change what you do in Section 2.

**2. Role tags tell you who does each step.** Many procedures put a letter or a word at the start of each step, such as [E] for electrician, [M] for millwright or [S] for supervisor. Only do the steps that are yours. Some steps are for everyone.

**3. Hold points.** A hold point is a step you must not go past until something is done or someone signs. Look for words like "Do not go on to…", "Stop", "until" and "only then".

**4. Conditions and loops.** "If…" steps tell you what to do when something is not normal. Often they send you back to an earlier step, such as locking out again.

**5. Lockout appears more than once.** In BC, the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation (Part 10) says that when machinery is shut down for maintenance, which includes installing, the energy isolating devices must be locked out before work is done. Lockout means using locks to make the machine inoperable or to isolate the energy, **following a written procedure**. Each worker's personal lock must be marked or tagged with who put it on. Each worker checks that the lockout works before starting, and removes their own lock when their work is done. In a commissioning procedure, lockout shows up every time someone works on the machine, and it comes off only for the tests. **Always follow your employer's own lockout procedure.**

**6. Records.** Commissioning steps often say "record" or "sign". The readings written down at start-up become the starting point for maintenance later.

### Worked example

**Question:** In Document A, who does step 2.1, and what must be true before Section 2 starts?

1. Step 2.1 starts with **[E]**. The key at the top says [E] is the licensed electrician.
2. Look back at Section 1. Step 1.3 says everyone locks out the conveyor following procedure LO-01 and checks the lockout **before any work starts**.
3. **Answer:** the electrician does step 2.1, and only after the start-up meeting (1.1), reading the procedure and manual (1.2) and the lockout and check (1.3) are done.

## Practice document

**Document A**

> **Fraserline Process Ltd. (fictional company) · Procedure CP-12: Commissioning a new conveyor drive motor**
>
> **Who does what:** [E] licensed electrician · [M] millwright · [S] supervisor · [All] everyone on the job
> This is a training procedure for a fictional company. It does not replace your employer's procedures or the manufacturer's manual.
>
> **Section 1. Before you start**
> 1.1 [S] Hold a start-up meeting. Check that the motor nameplate matches the drawing and the purchase order.
> 1.2 [All] Read this whole procedure and the motor manufacturer's manual.
> 1.3 [All] Lock out the conveyor following Fraserline lockout procedure LO-01. Each worker puts on their own tagged lock. Check that the lockout works before any work starts.
>
> **Section 2. Power path**
> 2.1 [E] Check that the overload relay and the fuses or breaker in the starter are the ones shown on the electrical drawing for this motor.
> 2.2 [E] Connect the motor leads as shown on the wiring diagram. Close and latch the panel.
> 2.3 [E] Rotation check. The motor must **not** be coupled to the gearbox yet, so a wrong direction cannot drive the conveyor backward. Clear everyone from the motor. Remove locks following LO-01. Start the motor for one second and stop it. Watch which way the shaft turns.
> 2.4 [E] If the direction is wrong, lock out again following LO-01 before you change any wiring. Then repeat 2.3.
>
> **Section 3. Alignment**
> 3.1 [All] Lock out again following LO-01. Check that the lockout works.
> 3.2 [M] Set the motor on its base. Check each foot for soft foot (a foot that does not sit flat on the base) and correct it with shims **before** you start aligning.
> 3.3 [M] Align the motor to the gearbox: up and down with shims, side to side with the jack screws. Align to the limits in the manufacturer's manual. Record the final readings on form CP-12A.
> 3.4 [M] Install the coupling and the coupling guard. **Do not go on to Section 4 until the guard is installed.**
>
> **Section 4. Test run**
> 4.1 [S] Check that everyone is clear of the conveyor. Each worker removes their own lock following LO-01.
> 4.2 [S] One person stands at the emergency stop and stays within reach of it for the whole test.
> 4.3 [S] Bump test: start the motor and stop it right away. Everyone watches and listens for the wrong direction, noise, vibration or rubbing.
> 4.4 [All] If anything is not normal, press the emergency stop, lock out following LO-01 and tell the supervisor. Do not start the motor again until the cause is found and fixed.
> 4.5 [S] If the bump test is normal, run the conveyor empty for the time the supervisor writes on form CP-12A. Check for heat, noise and vibration, and record what you find.
>
> **Section 5. Hand-over**
> 5.1 [S] Sign form CP-12A. The readings on it are the starting point for future maintenance.
> 5.2 [S] Only then may production staff run the conveyor.
