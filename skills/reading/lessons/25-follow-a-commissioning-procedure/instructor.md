# Follow a commissioning procedure

Session 25 of 26 in the Reading lessons · 75 minutes

**Before the session:** print Document A for each learner, one page per learner if you can, so they can mark it up. Open the WorkSafeBC page for Part 10 of the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation (address under Sources) to show in step 2.2. If the shop has a motor, a pump or a fan on a stand, and a lockout station, use them to point at the parts the procedure names. Do not run equipment for this lesson. This lesson builds on Reading lesson 3 (Follow written instructions) and Reading lesson 8 (Use an equipment manual). Problem-solving lesson 5 also covers lockout.

## Opening (5 min)

Welcome learners. Ask: "A brand-new conveyor motor has just been set in place. What could go wrong the first time someone presses Start?" Write answers on the board: runs backward, shakes, catches someone's hand, trips the breaker, burns out. Then say: "Every one of those has a step in a written procedure meant to stop it. Today we read one, and find out who does what, when to stop, and when the locks go on and come off."

This is session 25 of 26 in the Reading lessons. It runs 75 minutes.

Tell learners clearly: this is a reading lesson on a fictional procedure. It is not lockout training and it does not qualify anyone to commission equipment. On a job, they follow their employer's procedures and their supervisor.

## Objective (3 min)

By the end of the session, learners will be able to:

- find the role tag, hold points, conditions and lockout steps in a written procedure (Identify the information)
- connect a step to the earlier step it depends on, and to the separate lockout procedure it names (Make connections between different parts of the document(s))
- put steps in order and say what to do when a test goes wrong (Understand and apply the information)
- judge a co-worker's shortcut against the procedure (Evaluate the document(s))

## Pre-assessment (4 min)

1. "What does 'lock out' mean on a job?"
2. "Why would a procedure tell an electrician and a millwright to do different steps?"

## Post-assessment (6 min)

1. "In Document A, who does step 3.3, and what do they record?" (The millwright. The final alignment readings, on form CP-12A.)
2. "The rotation is wrong in step 2.3. What is the very next thing the electrician does?" (Lock out again following LO-01, before changing any wiring. Step 2.4.)
3. "Name one hold point in Document A and say what must happen before you go past it."

## Summary (5 min)

During this session we covered:

- what commissioning is
- reading a whole procedure before starting
- role tags such as [E], [M] and [S]
- hold points and "If…" steps that loop back
- where lockout goes on and comes off in a commissioning procedure, and what BC's Regulation says about lockout
- records, and why start-up readings matter for maintenance
- checking a shortcut against the written procedure

Total time: 5 + 3 + 4 + 52 + 6 + 5 = 75 minutes.

### Notes for the instructor

- **Generic steps only.** Document A is a training text for a fictional company. Its steps are general on purpose. It gives no voltages, settings, run times or alignment values, because those come from the manufacturer's manual and the employer, and they differ for every machine. Do not add numbers from memory.
- **Lockout.** The lesson states only what the WorkSafeBC Regulation says and that the employer's written procedure must be followed. It does not teach a lockout sequence. If learners ask how to lock out a specific machine, the answer is: the employer's written procedure for that machine, and the supervisor.
- **Trade lines.** Learners in the electrical stream may ask which steps a millwright may legally do and the other way around. Do not answer from memory. Say that the job's procedure and supervisor assign the steps, and that the rules for each trade's scope come from the trade's own regulator and the employer.

## Sources

- The six Reading components and their wording, and the Reading proficiency levels 1 to 3. Government of Canada, "Skill components and proficiency levels", https://www.canada.ca/en/services/jobs/training/initiatives/skills-success/learning-steps.html, checked 2026-11-08.
- Lockout in BC. In Part 10 of the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation: section 10.1 defines "lockout" as the use of a lock or locks to render machinery or equipment inoperable or to isolate an energy source in accordance with a written procedure, and defines "maintenance" to include installing. Section 10.3(1) says no work may be done on machinery shut down for maintenance until the energy isolating devices have been locked out. Section 10.4(1) says lockout procedures must be made available to all workers who are required to work on the machinery or equipment, and 10.4(4) says each personal lock must be marked or tagged to identify the person applying it. Section 10.6(2) says a worker must verify that all energy sources have been effectively locked out before starting work. Section 10.7 says each worker locks out before starting work and removes their personal locks on completion of their work. WorkSafeBC, "OHS Regulation Part 10: De-energization and Lockout", https://www.worksafebc.com/en/law-policy/occupational-health-safety/searchable-ohs-regulation/ohs-regulation/part-10-de-energization-and-lockout, checked 2026-11-08.
- Fraserline Process Ltd., procedure CP-12, lockout procedure LO-01 and form CP-12A are fictional and were written for this lesson. The order of the work in Document A (power path and rotation check, then alignment with soft foot corrected first, then a supervised bump test with the emergency stop within reach, then hand-over with recorded readings) follows the structure of a process analysis written by the site owner as BCIT coursework. That text was rewritten here in general terms, with no quotations, no employer names and no technical values.
