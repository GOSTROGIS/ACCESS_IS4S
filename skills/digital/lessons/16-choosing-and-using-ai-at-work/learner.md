# Choosing and using AI at work

Product facts were checked against each company's own pages, and privacy guidance against the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada and canada.ca, on 2026-11-06. Lessons 11 to 15 come first.

## Why this matters on the job

Your employer may already have an AI assistant in Outlook, Teams, Gmail or Docs. You may use a different one on your phone at home. The tool you pick, the account you sign in with and what you paste into it all matter. A client's address or your employer's bid price in the wrong chatbot can break a privacy law or a company rule. A résumé bullet the AI made up can cost you the job when the interviewer asks about it.

All four can write and explain text and read files or images you give them. All four can be wrong. Features change often, so check the help page before you rely on one.

### How to choose for a trades task

1. **At work, use the tool your employer approved, in your work account.** Work versions come with data protections your personal account does not have. For example, Microsoft says Copilot Chat prompts and responses are not used to train its foundation models, Google says chats on a Gemini work account are not reviewed by people or used to improve its AI models, and OpenAI says it does not train on business workspace content by default.
2. **Go where your files already are.** If your crew uses Outlook and Teams, Copilot is already there. If the company runs on Gmail and Google Docs, Gemini is already there.
3. **Pick by the job.** Voice on a phone helps when your hands are busy. File upload helps with a long spec sheet. Web search with links helps with anything that changes.
4. **Check every answer** with the check-it loop from Lesson 11.

### What never to paste

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada says to limit the personal information you share with AI chatbots, not to share sensitive or identifying information, and not to share information about other people. On a job, that means never paste:

- **Your Social Insurance Number.** The Government of Canada says your SIN is confidential and to share it only when the law requires it. You do not have to give it to fill out a job application.
- **Health information**, yours or anyone else's, including injury or claim details.
- **Client information:** names, addresses, phone numbers, lockbox or door codes, photos that show a house number.
- **Your employer's private information:** bid prices, contracts, drawings marked confidential, passwords, security codes.
- **Coworkers' information**, and never photos or details of children.

Instead, take the details out. Write "Client A" or "a two-storey house in the Fraser Valley". The chatbot does not need the real name to help with the problem.

### Employer AI policies

Many employers now have written rules about AI. A policy usually says:

- which AI tools are approved
- which account to use (work, not personal)
- what you may and may not paste
- when you must say AI helped
- who checks AI work before it goes to a client

If there is no written policy, ask your supervisor before you use AI for work. Using your personal chatbot for company information can break a rule even if the tool is good.

### Citing and disclosing AI use

- **You own what you send.** "The AI said so" is not an excuse for a wrong quantity or a wrong rule.
- **Cite the real source, not the chatbot.** Write "WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation s. 11.2", not "ChatGPT".
- **Say when AI helped** if your employer's policy asks, or if the reader would expect to know. A short note is enough: "I used Gemini to help word this handout. I checked the rule on worksafebc.com."
- Some product terms say the same. Microsoft's Copilot Terms of Use tell users not to mislead others about whether they used Copilot to create their content.

### AI and job applications

AI can help you match your words to a job posting, fix spelling and practise interview answers. It cannot give you experience you do not have.

- **Everything must be true.** Check every skill, ticket, tool and date the AI writes. If you do not hold a ticket, it does not go on the résumé.
- **Keep private details out.** Do not paste your SIN, your references' phone numbers or your full address into a chatbot.
- **Be ready to explain it.** An interviewer will ask about anything on your résumé. If you cannot talk about it, take it off.
- **Follow the employer's instructions.** If an application asks whether you used AI, answer honestly.

### Worked example: tailor a résumé bullet, then check it is true

Kai is applying to Riverside Mechanical Ltd. (a fictional company) for a first-year plumbing apprentice job. The posting asks for "WHMIS training, PEX and copper experience, and the ability to read basic drawings".

**Kai's facts (his truth list):**

- Worked 3 months helping a family friend on a basement renovation
- Ran PEX water lines with supervision
- Has a WHMIS certificate
- Has not worked with copper
- Read the floor plan to find fixture locations

Kai's old bullet: "Helped with plumbing on a house reno."

Kai gives a chatbot his truth list and the posting and asks for one stronger bullet that uses only his facts. Suppose it writes:

"Installed PEX and copper water lines on a 3-month residential renovation, reading drawings to place fixtures, with WHMIS training."

Kai checks each claim against his truth list:

| Claim in the AI bullet | True? |
|---|---|
| Installed PEX lines | Yes, with supervision |
| Copper lines | **No** |
| 3-month residential renovation | Yes |
| Read drawings to place fixtures | Yes, the floor plan |
| WHMIS training | Yes |

Kai's final bullet: "Ran PEX water lines under supervision on a 3-month basement renovation, using the floor plan to locate fixtures. WHMIS certified."

The AI made the wording stronger. Kai made it true.
