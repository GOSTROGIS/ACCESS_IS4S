Put your community on the map

Find a place that matters to you and the training site on a map, say how far apart they are and in which direction, read a map coloured by numbers, and decide whose data a map shows and who gets to share it.

Why this matters on the job

Many trades jobs are away from home: camp jobs, a pipeline spread, a mill shutdown in another town. Knowing how far a job is from your community, and in which direction, helps you plan travel and talk with an employer about getting home. Reports and funding proposals also use maps coloured by numbers. You should be able to read one and ask two questions: what does it leave out, and whose information is it?

Key ideas

You choose what to share

For this lesson, pick a place that matters to you: your home community, your Nation's community, a town where you grew up or worked, or a public landmark. You decide what goes on the screen. You never have to show where you live, and no home address goes on a shared screen.

Which way is it? Read the coordinates

Right-click a place in Google Maps on a computer to see its latitude and longitude (Lesson 9). Then compare two places:

  • North or south: the place with the bigger latitude is farther north.
  • East or west: in BC, longitudes are negative. The more negative longitude is farther west. For example, −124.5 is west of −122.9.
  • Put the two together: north-west, south-east, and so on. If one of the differences is very small, use only the other direction.

How far is it? Measure a straight line

Right-click the first place, choose Measure distance, and click the second place (Lesson 9). This is the straight-line distance. A road, ferry or flight is longer.

Worked example

Vancouver City Hall is at 49.2608, -123.1139. BC Place stadium is at 49.2767, -123.1119.

  1. Latitude: 49.2767 is bigger than 49.2608, so BC Place is north of City Hall.
  2. Longitude: −123.1119 is a little less negative than −123.1139, so BC Place is a little east. The difference is tiny, only 0.0020 of a degree, compared with 0.0159 of a degree north. So the direction is simply north.
  3. Measure distance from City Hall to BC Place: about 1.8 km.
  4. Sentence: BC Place is about 1.8 km north of Vancouver City Hall.

Maps coloured by numbers

A choropleth map shades each area by a number, such as a count of people. The legend shows which shade means which range of numbers. On the demo map below, more ink means a bigger count.

The map and its data are fictional. Granite Valley is not a real place, and no real people are counted.

What a choropleth map cannot tell you:

  • where inside an area the people are
  • anything about one person
  • whether an area has a big count only because a lot of people live there

To compare areas fairly, divide the count by the population. That gives a rate, for example "per 1,000 people". A big area with many people can have the biggest count and still a low rate.

Whose data is it?

Information about people belongs first to those people. Before you put anyone's community, name or photo on a map that others will see, ask them. Information about learners in a training program is private, which is why this lesson uses fictional data.

For information about First Nations, the First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC) sets out the First Nations principles of OCAP®: ownership, control, access and possession. FNIGC explains that these principles say how First Nations' data and information will be collected, protected, used or shared. FNIGC also says OCAP® is a set of First Nations principles, not general Indigenous ones, and that each First Nation may interpret them in its own way. Read FNIGC's own explanation at https://fnigc.ca/ocap-training/.

OCAP® is a registered trademark of the First Nations Information Governance Centre (FNIGC).

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) In Google Maps on a computer, find the training site and the place you chose. Right-click each one and write its latitude and longitude to 4 decimal places. Write only a place name you are comfortable sharing.

Training site: ___ Your place: ___

Task 2 (level 1) Compare the two latitudes, then the two longitudes. Is your place north or south of the training site? East or west? Write the direction in one or two words.

Task 3 (level 2) Measure the straight-line distance from the training site to your place. Write a sentence like the one in the worked example. Then ask Google Maps for driving directions, or write how people usually travel there if there is no road. Explain why the travel distance is longer than the straight line.

Task 4 (level 1) Use the demo map and its legend. (a) Which district has the most ink, and what is its count? (b) Which two districts are in the 0 to 9 range? (c) Which range is River Bend in?

(a) __ (b) _ (c) ___

Task 5 (level 2) Here are the fictional populations of Granite Valley. Work out the rate per 1,000 people for each district: count ÷ population × 1,000. Which district has the highest rate? Does the map's shading show that?

District Apprentices Population Rate per 1,000
North Ridge 12 4,000
Lake District 27 9,000
East Hills 7 3,500
Cedar Flats 4 1,000
River Bend 18 6,000
Harbour 41 20,500

Task 6 (level 2) A classmate wants to make a graduation slideshow with a map that puts a pin on every learner's home community, with each learner's name and photo beside the pin. Write three things that should happen, or questions that should be asked, before that map is made or shown.

Task 7 (level 2) Tell a partner your sentence from Task 3 and one thing the demo map cannot tell you. Your partner repeats your sentence back.

Check yourself

  1. Two BC places have longitudes −121.9 and −126.3. Which one is farther west?
  2. What does the legend on a choropleth map tell you?
  3. Why is a rate per 1,000 people sometimes fairer than a count?
  4. What do the letters in OCAP® stand for?
  5. Before you put someone's community on a map others will see, what do you do first?
