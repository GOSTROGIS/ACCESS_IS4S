Search and judge online information

Search with quotes, site and minus to get better results, read a web address to see who really runs the page, and check whether information is current before you rely on it.

Why this matters on the job

Before you start a job you may need the current safety rule, the right form, a supplier's spec sheet or an employer's application page. The first search result is not always the right one: it can be an old copy, a forum post, an ad or a fake site built to look official. In the trades, acting on the wrong version of a rule can cost money or get someone hurt.

Key ideas

Read the web address

A web address (URL) is the location of a page. Example:

https://www.worksafebc.com/en/health-safety/hazards-exposures/ergonomics

  • https:// means the connection is encrypted. It does not prove the site is honest.
  • www.worksafebc.com is the domain: who runs the site.
  • Everything after the first single / is the page inside that site.

To find the real owner, look at the part just before the first single /, and read it from the right. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security warns that fake sites use addresses like login.google.com.example.com. That page belongs to example.com, not Google.

Examples of official addresses you will use in these lessons:

Address ends in Who runs it
canada.ca or .gc.ca (for example cyber.gc.ca) Government of Canada
gov.bc.ca Government of British Columbia
worksafebc.com WorkSafeBC
vancouver.ca City of Vancouver

Search smarter

Google Search Help lists these operators. Do not put a space after the colon.

Type this It finds Example
"words in quotes" that exact phrase "confined space"
site: results only from one site ladder safety site:worksafebc.com
-word results without that word pipe threading -plastic
filetype: one kind of file computer workstation filetype:pdf
before: and after: pages updated before or after a date scaffold after:2025

Judge what you find

Ask five questions about any page:

  1. Who made it? Look at the domain and the "About" page.
  2. When was it updated? Government of Canada pages show "Date modified" at the bottom. Many guides show a publication date.
  3. Does it answer my question, for BC, for my trade, today?
  4. Is it the source, or someone repeating the source? A blog about a WorkSafeBC rule is not the rule.
  5. Can I confirm it in a second trusted place?

Some results are paid ads, and they can look like normal results. Check the label and the address before you click.

Worked example

Kai needs WorkSafeBC's guide on setting up a computer workstation.

  1. Kai searches computer workstation guide site:worksafebc.com. Every result is now from WorkSafeBC.
  2. Kai opens How to Make Your Computer Workstation Fit You.
  3. Kai checks the address starts with www.worksafebc.com/ and reads the publication details: May 2023, publication BK76.
  4. Kai writes a source note: title, publisher, date, address, date checked.

Task 3 (level 2) Search computer workstation fit you site:worksafebc.com. Find the guide in the worked example. Write its publication date and publication number.

Task 4 (level 2) You search for a fall protection rule and get these three results. Rank them from most to least trustworthy for a BC job site and give a reason for each.

Task 6 (level 2) Run the same search twice and compare the first three results: ladder and then ladder inspection site:worksafebc.com. Write what changed and why.

Check yourself

  1. Does https:// prove a site is official?
  2. Who really owns login.google.com.example.com?
  3. What does site:worksafebc.com do in a search?
  4. Where do Government of Canada pages show when they were last changed?
  5. Why is a blog about a safety rule weaker evidence than the rule itself?
