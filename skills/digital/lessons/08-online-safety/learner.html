Passwords, passkeys and phishing

Make strong passphrases, use multi-factor authentication and passkeys, spot phishing emails, texts, calls and QR codes, and know where to report a scam.

Guidance checked against the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (cyber.gc.ca) and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, 2026-11-06. It applies to any phone, tablet or computer.

Why this matters on the job

Your work email, pay stub site, union account and ticket records all sit behind a sign-in. If someone steals that sign-in, they can redirect your pay, send fake orders in your name or lock you out. Scammers also target trades workers with fake job offers, fake supplier invoices and texts that look like they come from payroll. A few habits stop most of this.

Key ideas

Passphrases and passwords

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (the Cyber Centre) recommends a passphrase: a string of mixed words you can remember.

  • At least 4 words and 15 characters, with or without spaces.
  • One way to build one: look around a room and pick unrelated words for things you see.
  • If a site will not take a passphrase, use a password of at least 12 characters with upper and lower case letters, numbers and special characters.

The Cyber Centre also says:

  • Use a different passphrase for each account, especially email and banking.
  • Do not share it, even with family. Do not give it out by phone or online.
  • Do not use your birthday, hometown, pet's name, song lyrics, or anything like password or 1234, even with tricks like p@ssword.
  • Do not type passwords on public Wi-Fi.
  • Sign out when you finish on a shared computer.

A password manager can create and remember passwords for you. The Cyber Centre says to protect it with a strong passphrase and multi-factor authentication, keep it updated, and not use it for your most sensitive accounts, such as banking.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

MFA means you need two or more different things to sign in:

  • something you know (a passphrase or PIN)
  • something you have (your phone or a security key)
  • something you are (a fingerprint or face)

So a stolen password alone is not enough. Turn MFA on for email, banking and any work account. If you get a sign-in approval request you did not start, deny it: it may mean someone is trying to access your account. Treat it as suspicious and report it; the prompt alone does not prove how it was triggered. Some MFA asks you to match a number shown on screen, which helps stop that trick. The Cyber Centre suggests avoiding text-message (SMS) codes as an MFA method where a better option is offered.

Passkeys

A passkey replaces the password. It is a digital key stored on your device and unlocked with your fingerprint, face or PIN. The website never gets a password to steal, and passkeys resist phishing because a fake site cannot use them. Many accounts still keep a password as a backup way in, so that password still needs to be strong.

Phishing

Phishing is a message that looks real but is built to make you give up information, click a bad link, open a bad attachment or send money. It comes as:

  • email (phishing)
  • text message (smishing)
  • phone call (vishing), sometimes with an AI-made voice that sounds like someone you know
  • QR code (quishing), on a poster, a parking meter or in an email

The Cyber Centre says something may be "phishy" if:

  • it is urgent and has a deadline
  • it asks for personal or confidential information
  • it asks you to sign in through a link it gives you
  • the offer sounds too good to be true
  • you did not ask for it and it has an attachment, link, QR code or sign-in page, or claims to be the government or a bank
  • you do not recognize the sender. Addresses can be faked, so a known name is not proof.

Spelling mistakes used to be a giveaway. With AI, many scam messages are now well written, so check the other signs.

What to do

  1. Stay calm. Scams depend on rushing you.
  2. Do not click, scan, reply or call the number in the message.
  3. Check through a separate channel. If "the bank" calls, hang up and call your branch from the number on your card or the bank's website. If "payroll" emails, phone or walk over to payroll.
  4. Report it. At work, tell your supervisor or IT. For fraud, report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or 1-888-495-8501. If you lost money or information, also contact your local police as soon as possible.

Worked example

Tanis gets this text: RIVERBEND PAYROLL: Your direct deposit failed. Update your bank info in 2 hrs or miss this pay: [link].

Signs: urgent deadline, asks for banking information, sends a sign-in link, unexpected. Tanis does not tap the link. She phones the Riverbend office number on her pay stub. Payroll says no message was sent. Tanis forwards a screenshot to her supervisor and deletes the text.

Practice

Never type a real password, PIN or security answer in class. Use invented examples only.

Task 1 (level 1) Build a practice passphrase using the room method. Write four unrelated words for things you can see in this room, then count the characters including spaces. Do not use it for a real account.

Words: _____ Characters: ____

Task 2 (level 1) Rate each practice password as strong or weak, using the Cyber Centre guidance, and give one reason.

  • Fluffy2009 ____
  • p@ssword123 ____
  • gravel kettle spruce lantern ____
  • 1234 ____

Task 3 (level 2) Your phone shows a sign-in approval request for your work email at 2:00 a.m. You were asleep and did not try to sign in. What do you do, and what does the request tell you?

Task 4 (level 2) List every phishing sign in this fictional email.

From: Riverbend Builders HR <hr-riverbend@quick-mail-help.example> Subject: FINAL NOTICE: confirm your SIN today

Your employment file is incomplete. Click the secure link below and enter your Social Insurance Number and banking details within 24 hours or your position will be cancelled.

Task 5 (level 2) A poster on the site trailer door says Scan for your new safety orientation schedule with a QR code taped over another code. What could be wrong, and what would you do instead?

Task 6 (level 1) A caller says they are from your bank and asks you to read back a code that was just texted to you. Write the steps you take, in order.

Check yourself

  1. How long should a passphrase be, according to the Cyber Centre?
  2. Name the three kinds of factors in multi-factor authentication.
  3. Why can a fake website not steal a passkey the way it steals a password?
  4. Name three signs that a message may be phishing.
  5. Where do you report a fraud in Canada?
