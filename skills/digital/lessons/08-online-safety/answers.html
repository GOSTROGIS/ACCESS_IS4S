Passwords, passkeys and phishing

Make strong passphrases, use multi-factor authentication and passkeys, spot phishing emails, texts, calls and QR codes, and know where to report a scam.

  • Learner
  • Instructor
  • Answers
  • Level: 1-2
  • Time: 60 min
  • Components: Apply safe and responsible practices online; Use online tools and platforms

Task 1 (level 1)

Open answer. A good response has four unrelated words and a character count of 15 or more, counting spaces.

Worked check with the example gravel kettle spruce lantern: 6 + 6 + 6 + 7 = 25 letters, plus 3 spaces = 28 characters. Four words and 28 characters meets the guidance. (This example is now printed in a lesson, so nobody should use it for a real account.)

Task 2 (level 1)

  • Fluffy2009: weak. It looks like a pet's name and a year, which the Cyber Centre says not to use, and it is only 10 characters.
  • p@ssword123: weak. It is "password" with a character swap, which the Cyber Centre names as a mistake.
  • gravel kettle spruce lantern: strong as a pattern. Four unrelated words, 28 characters. (Weak now that it is published.)
  • 1234: weak. It is a common, easily guessed number and far shorter than 12 characters.

Task 3 (level 2)

Answer: deny the request. Do not approve it to make it go away. The request means someone else has your password and is trying to sign in. Change your work email password (from a device you trust, through the normal sign-in page, not a link), and tell IT or your supervisor the same day.

Level 2 because the learner has to work out what the request means, not only what button to press.

Task 4 (level 2)

Signs:

  • The sender name says Riverbend Builders HR, but the address is quick-mail-help.example, not a company address.
  • The subject shouts FINAL NOTICE and the body sets a 24-hour deadline (urgency).
  • It asks for your SIN and banking details (personal and confidential information).
  • It asks you to click a link to enter them.
  • It threatens that your position will be cancelled (fear).
  • It was not expected.

What to do: do not click. Phone or visit HR using a number or office you already know. Report it to your supervisor or IT.

Task 5 (level 2)

Answer: a code taped over another code is a sign of quishing. The new QR code may send you to a fake page that asks for a sign-in or personal details. Do not scan it. Ask the site supervisor or safety coordinator for the schedule, or go to the company's real site by typing the address you already know. Tell the supervisor about the poster so it can be removed.

Task 6 (level 1)

Steps in order:

  1. Do not read the code out. It is a sign-in code meant only for you, and giving it away lets the caller into your account.
  2. Hang up.
  3. Call the bank using the number on your card or the bank's own website, not a number the caller gave.
  4. Tell the bank what happened.
  5. Report the call to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or 1-888-495-8501).

Accept small differences in order if steps 1 to 3 come first.

Check yourself

  1. At least 4 words and at least 15 characters.
  2. Something you know, something you have, something you are.
  3. A passkey's private key stays on your device and the credential is unique to each real website, so the fake site has no password to collect and cannot use the passkey.
  4. Any three of: urgent deadline, asks for personal or confidential information, asks you to sign in through its link, too good to be true, unexpected with an attachment, link, QR code or sign-in page, claims to be the government or a bank, unknown sender.
  5. The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, online at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or by phone at 1-888-495-8501, and local police if you lost money or information.
