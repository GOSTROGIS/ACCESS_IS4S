Spot waste on the job

Walk a work area, spot waiting, extra trips, rework and extra stock, measure one waste, and pick the one most worth fixing.

  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Identify opportunities for you to innovate; Use your imagination and curiosity; Facilitate a creative and innovative environment for yourself and others

Why this matters on the job

On a busy site, a lot of time goes into things that do not build anything: walking to the van, waiting for a lift, cutting a piece again, moving a pile of material out of the way for the third time. Nobody plans this waste. It just builds up. Construction companies that use Lean methods send people on a waste walk to see it. You do not need a title to do a waste walk. An apprentice who notices waste, measures it and suggests a fix is showing exactly the kind of thinking a foreman wants.

Key ideas

Waste is anything that uses time, effort or material without adding value for the customer. Toyota engineer Taiichi Ohno named seven kinds of waste for factories. Construction groups use a version with eight. This lesson uses four that are easy to see on any site.

  • Waiting: people stand by for a lift, a key, a decision, a delivery or another trade.
  • Extra trips: walking or driving to get tools, parts or drawings, or moving material more than once.
  • Rework: doing work again because it was wrong the first time.
  • Extra stock: more material on site than the job needs right now. It gets in the way, gets damaged and has to be moved.

A waste walk. Pick an area and a time, for example one room for one hour. Walk it, or stand where you can see it. Watch the work, not the people. Use a tally sheet: one mark each time you see a waste. Write a short note on the biggest ones.

Watch the process, not the person. "The wire is 45 m away, so people walk a lot" is about the process. "Sam is slow" is about a person. Waste walks only work when people trust you are not there to blame them. Ask the crew what slows them down. They know.

Safety comes first. Wear your PPE. Stay in areas you are allowed to be in. Never stand in the way of moving equipment to watch.

Pick one waste to fix. You will see more waste than you can fix. Score each one, for example on time lost, how often it happens and how easy it is to fix, and pick the top one. Then write an opportunity statement (lesson 2) and measure the fix (lesson 11).

Worked example

Coastline Electrical Contractors (a fictional company) has two electricians roughing in a clinic renovation on Harbour Road. Lena, a second-year apprentice, does a waste walk for 2 hours one morning.

Tally. Lena's tally sheet after 2 hours:

Waste Tally Notes
Waiting 3 Waited for drywallers to clear Exam 2, 35 minutes in total
Extra trips 9 Trips to the van for wire, boxes and fittings, about 3 minutes a round trip
Rework 2 Two boxes set at the wrong height and moved, 20 minutes in total
Extra stock 1 Three reels of wire in the corridor, not used today, moved twice

Measure the biggest one. Trips to the van happen the most. 9 trips in 2 hours is 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 trips an hour. In an 8-hour day that is 4.5 × 8 = 36 trips. At 3 minutes each, that is 36 × 3 = 108 minutes a day of walking.

Score and choose.

Waste Time lost (1 to 3) How often (1 to 3) Easy to fix (1 to 3) Total
Waiting for drywall 2 1 1 4
Trips to the van 3 3 3 9
Box height rework 1 1 2 4
Extra reels in the corridor 1 1 3 5

Trips to the van score highest.

Opportunity statement. "How might we keep the day's wire and fittings close to the work, and still keep them secure?"

Idea. The empty storage room on site can be used for material. Each morning, one trip brings the day's material from the van into the storage room.

A round trip to the storage room takes about 0.5 minutes. The new daily time for 36 trips is 36 × 0.5 = 18 minutes. The saving is 108 − 18 = 90 minutes a day, which is 90 × 5 = 450 minutes, or 7.5 hours, in a 5-day week.

These are estimates: they assume the two-hour trip rate continues for the full day. The 90-minute saving is before the time needed to bring the day's material into the storage room. Measure that stocking time and the actual trips during the trial, then subtract the stocking time to find the net saving.

Lena brings her tally sheet and the numbers to her foreman. The foreman checks with the general contractor that the storage room can be used, and the crew tries it the next week.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Name the waste: waiting, extra trips, rework or extra stock.

  1. A pipefitter walks back to the shop for a forgotten fitting.
  2. A paving crew stands by for 20 minutes because the next truck is late.
  3. A carpenter recuts a rafter that was cut 50 mm short.
  4. Forty sheets of drywall are delivered two weeks early and block the hallway.
  5. An ironworker waits for the crane to finish another lift.
  6. A welder grinds out and redoes a weld that failed inspection.
  7. A crew moves a pallet of blocks three times to get it out of the way.

Task 2 (level 1) Look at Lena's "before" floor plan. Where is the van parked? Which room is empty? Where are the unused reels?

Task 3 (level 2) On a framing site, a crew of 3 makes 5 trips each an hour to a nail and hanger bin 60 m away. A round trip takes 2 minutes. Use BEDMAS and show your work.

a) How many minutes a day does the crew spend on these trips in an 8-hour day?

b) How many hours is that in a 5-day week?

Task 4 (level 2) The bin in Task 3 is moved next to the work. A round trip now takes 0.5 minutes. How many minutes a day does the crew save?

Task 5 (level 2) A tally sheet from 1 hour at a pipe prefab shop shows: waiting 4, extra trips 11, rework 2, extra stock 3.

a) Which waste happened most often?

b) Draw a simple bar chart of the tally.

c) Why might the most frequent waste not be the most costly? Give an example.

Task 6 (level 2) A carpenter's cut list had a wrong length, so 6 stair treads were cut 15 mm short. Each tread board costs $18 and takes 12 minutes to cut and fit.

a) What did the rework cost in material?

b) How many minutes of work were lost?

c) What check could stop this happening again?

Task 7 (level 3) Score these four wastes from a drywall job from 1 to 3 on time lost, how often and easy to fix. Pick one to fix and explain your choice.

  • The crew waits about 15 minutes most mornings for the hoist.
  • Screws run out about once a week, and someone drives to the supplier (1 hour).
  • About one sheet a day is cut wrong and recut.
  • Mud buckets are stored on the first floor, but the work is on the fourth.
Waste Time lost How often Easy to fix Total

Task 8 (level 3) For the waste you picked in Task 7:

a) Write an opportunity statement that starts with "How might we ...". Do not put the answer in it.

b) Say what you would measure before and after the fix, and how (lesson 11).

Task 9 (level 2) Rewrite each comment so it is about the process, not the person.

  1. "Mike is always slow getting set up."
  2. "The new guy keeps cutting stuff wrong."
  3. "Nobody on this crew can find anything."

Task 10 (level 3) Plan a 20-minute waste walk of your classroom, shop or a workplace you know. Write:

  • the area and the time
  • what you will watch for
  • the safety rules you will follow
  • who you will ask what slows them down
  • how you will share what you find without blaming anyone

Check yourself

  1. Name the four wastes in this lesson.
  2. What is a waste walk?
  3. Why should you watch the process and not the person?
  4. 4 trips an hour, 3 minutes each, in an 8-hour day. How many minutes a day is that?
  5. After you find a waste worth fixing, what are the next two steps?
