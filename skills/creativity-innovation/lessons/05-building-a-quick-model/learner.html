Building a quick model and explaining it

Turn an idea into a quick model with building bricks, then give spoken directions clear enough for someone else to build it.

  • Components: Develop your ideas; Apply your ideas

Why this matters on the job

An idea is not much use on a crew until someone else can build it. A lead hand explains a layout. A millwright explains how a guard goes back on. You explain the jig you thought up. A quick, rough model shows the idea faster than words, and trying to explain the model shows you which details you have not worked out yet. Models made from cheap materials let you find problems before you spend real time and material.

Key ideas

A quick model (prototype) is a rough version of an idea, made fast from cheap materials such as building bricks, cardboard or scrap. It does not need to look good. It needs to show how the idea works.

Developing an idea means adding the details other people need to build it:

  • what: which piece, by size, shape and colour ("the red brick with 2 rows of 4 bumps")
  • how many: a count, not "some"
  • where: position from a fixed point ("on the left end, from where you are sitting")
  • which way: flat or on edge, long side toward you or away
  • in what order: one step at a time, with words like first, next, then, last

Whose left? When two people sit back to back, your left is their left. When they face each other, your left is their right. Say "from where you are sitting."

Expect it to go wrong the first time. The first set of directions almost always misses something. That is the point of the test. You fix the directions and try again.

Worked example

Tess builds a small model of a tool caddy from 10 bricks. Her partner sits back to back with her and has the same 10 bricks.

First try, no questions allowed: "Put the long red one on top." Her partner puts it on top, but across the caddy instead of along it, and on the wrong end.

What was missing: which way the brick faces, and where on the top it goes.

Second try: "Take the red brick with 2 rows of 8 bumps. Lay it flat with the long side toward you. Put it across the top of the two grey walls so it lines up with the left end, from where you are sitting." This time it matches.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) With your bag of 10 to 12 bricks, build a quick model of something useful on a job site, such as a tool caddy, a sawhorse, a pipe rack or a material cart. Use every brick. Do not show your partner.

Task 2 (level 1) Round 1. Sit back to back with your partner. Give spoken directions so your partner can build a copy of your model. Your partner may not ask questions or look. When you finish, turn around and compare the two models.

Task 3 (level 2) List what went wrong in Round 1. For each problem, write which kind of detail was missing: what, how many, where, which way or in what order.

What went wrong Missing detail

Task 4 (level 2) Round 2. Take your partner's model apart. Give your directions again with the missing details added. This time your partner may ask questions. Compare. Then switch roles and do both rounds with your partner's model.

Task 5 (level 2) Write the build steps for your model as a numbered list of 5 to 8 steps. Use order words and the five kinds of detail. Someone who was not in the room should be able to build it from your steps.

Check yourself

  1. What is a quick model, and why make it from cheap materials?
  2. Name the five kinds of detail that help someone build from your directions.
  3. Two people face each other across a bench. You say "on the left." Whose left will your partner probably use?
  4. Why do the first directions usually go wrong, and what do you do about it?
