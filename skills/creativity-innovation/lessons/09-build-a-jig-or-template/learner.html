Build a jig or template

Design a drilling jig for a repeated shop task, test it as a cardboard prototype, then work out how much time it saves.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Apply your ideas; Develop your ideas; Generate ideas that are novel to yourself or others

Why this matters on the job

When a task repeats 20, 50 or 200 times, measuring each one from scratch is slow and every measurement is a new chance for a mistake. A jig or template is something you make once so the task comes out the same every time. Cabinetmakers use them for shelf-pin holes. Framers use story sticks and stop blocks. Welders use fixtures to hold parts in the same place. Pipefitters use spacer blocks. In lesson 3 you came up with jig ideas. Today you design one, test it as a cheap prototype, and prove what it saves.

Key ideas

Jig or template. A template shows you where to mark or cut. A jig also holds or guides the tool. Many shop aids do both.

Locate from a fixed edge. A good jig has a fence or stop that sits against an edge of the work. Then every hole is measured from the same place, every time.

Prototype first. Build a quick, cheap version from cardboard or scrap (lesson 5). Test it. Fix what fails. Only then build the real one from plywood or steel.

Know the standard you are working to. Many cabinets are built to the 32 mm system, which is used around the world:

  • shelf-pin holes are 5 mm in diameter
  • holes in a row are 32 mm apart, centre to centre
  • the front row of holes is 37 mm from the front edge of the cabinet side

Is it worth building? Compare the time saved with the time it takes to make the jig.

  • time saved = (old time per piece − new time per piece) × number of pieces
  • net time saved = time saved − time to build the jig
  • break-even = time to build the jig ÷ time saved per piece. Past this number of pieces, the jig has paid for itself.

Safety with a drill and jig. These steps come from the Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety:

  • wear safety glasses
  • clamp the work and the jig. Never hold small stock with one hand while you drill with the other
  • tighten the chuck and remove the chuck key before you start
  • unplug the drill or remove the battery before you change a bit
  • do not reach under or around the stock being drilled

Worked example

Westshore Millwork (a fictional shop) is building 12 cabinet sides. Each side needs two rows of shelf-pin holes, front and back, with 9 holes in each row. Right now an apprentice marks every hole with a tape and square, punches it and drills it. That takes about 1.2 minutes a hole.

Generate. The crew lists ideas: a drilled steel bar, a cardboard template, a story stick, a plywood strip with a fence, a bought jig. They pick the plywood strip with a fence, because it uses shop scrap and anyone can make it.

Design. The jig is a strip of 12 mm plywood with a fence along one edge. The fence rides on the cabinet's front edge, so every hole lands 37 mm in from that edge.

Hole positions along the strip, measured from the left end:

  • hole 1 is 22 mm from the end
  • each next hole is 32 mm further: 22, 54, 86, 118, 150, 182, 214, 246, 278
  • span from hole 1 to hole 9: 8 gaps × 32 = 256 mm, and 22 + 256 = 278 ✓
  • strip length: 22 + 256 + 22 = 300 mm

Prototype. The apprentice draws the layout on cardboard and pokes the holes with an awl. She lays it on a scrap side and marks through it. She measures from hole 1 to hole 9: 259 mm, not 256. The cardboard holes wander. The idea works, but cardboard is not accurate enough. She builds the plywood version on the drill press, and it measures 256 mm.

Measure the saving. With the jig, each hole takes about 0.5 minutes.

  • holes in the job: 12 sides × 2 rows × 9 holes = 216 holes
  • old time: 216 × 1.2 = 259.2 minutes
  • new time: 216 × 0.5 = 108 minutes
  • time saved: 259.2 − 108 = 151.2 minutes, or (1.2 − 0.5) × 216 = 0.7 × 216 = 151.2 ✓
  • time to make the jig: 15 minutes for the cardboard prototype + 45 minutes for the plywood jig = 60 minutes
  • net time saved: 151.2 − 60 = 91.2 minutes, about 1.5 hours on this job alone

Break-even. One side has 18 holes, so the jig saves 0.7 × 18 = 12.6 minutes a side. 60 ÷ 12.6 = 4.76. After 5 sides the jig has paid for itself. The other 7 sides are pure saving, and the jig is ready for the next job.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Use the jig drawing.

a) How far apart are the holes?

b) How far is the row of holes from the fence face?

c) How many holes does the jig have, and what size are they?

Task 2 (level 2) A second jig has 7 holes on 32 mm centres. The first hole is 20 mm from the end, and the strip has the same 20 mm past the last hole.

a) List the distance of each hole from the left end.

b) How long is the strip?

Task 3 (level 2) A new order has 8 cabinet sides. Each side has two rows of 11 holes. Marking and drilling by hand takes 1.2 minutes a hole. With the jig it takes 0.5 minutes a hole. Use BEDMAS and show your work.

a) How many holes are in the order?

b) How many minutes does the jig save?

Task 4 (level 2) The jig took 60 minutes to make. Each side in Task 3 has 22 holes.

a) How many minutes does the jig save on one side?

b) After how many sides has the jig paid for itself? Round up to a whole side.

Task 5 (level 2) A metal fab shop must drill two 14 mm holes in each of 60 steel angle clips. The holes are 50 mm apart and 25 mm from the end of the clip. Right now each clip is laid out with a rule, scribe and centre punch. Generate at least 6 different ideas for a jig or fixture. Do not judge them yet. Use at least two prompts from lesson 3 (do it differently, ask a question, combine, reverse, expand).

Task 6 (level 2) Choose one idea from Task 5. Write a prototype plan:

  • what you will make first, from what cheap material
  • what you will test, and how you will measure it
  • what result would tell you to build the real one

Task 7 (level 3) Westshore gets a job with taller cabinet sides. Shelf holes may only go between 150 mm and 750 mm up from the bottom of the side. The holes must be on 32 mm centres.

a) What is the most holes that fit in one row? Show your reasoning.

b) Centre the row in the zone so the space left over is equal at the top and bottom. How high is the first hole, and how high is the last hole?

c) The 9-hole jig cannot do this row in one placement. Describe how the crew could use it anyway.

Task 8 (level 3) A cardboard prototype of an 11-hole jig measures 324 mm from the first hole to the last. It should measure 320 mm.

a) How many gaps are between 11 holes? What should each gap be?

b) What is the average error per gap?

c) What would you change for the next version, and how would you check it?

Task 9 (level 2) Mark each statement S (safe) or U (unsafe) for drilling with a jig.

  1. Hold the jig on the board with one hand and drill with the other.
  2. Clamp the jig and the work to the bench.
  3. Leave the chuck key in so it is handy.
  4. Remove the battery before changing the bit.
  5. Wear safety glasses.
  6. Reach under the board to feel if the bit is through.

Task 10 (level 3) Write a two-minute pitch for the Westshore jig, using the six parts from lesson 7: problem, idea, test, cost and time, safety, ask. Use the numbers from the worked example.

Check yourself

  1. What is the difference between a template and a jig?
  2. Why does a good jig have a fence or stop?
  3. In the 32 mm system, what size are shelf-pin holes, how far apart are they, and how far is the front row from the front edge?
  4. A jig takes 30 minutes to make and saves 6 minutes a piece. What is the break-even?
  5. Why build a cardboard prototype before the plywood jig?
