Testing an idea and learning from what fails

Build a cardboard bracket, load-test it, and use each failure to make the next version better.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Apply your ideas; Develop your ideas

Why this matters on the job

On the job, ideas get tested every day: a temporary support, a new way to rig a load, a different layout for a pipe rack. Many first tries do not work. Good tradespeople expect that, find out exactly how and why the idea failed, change one thing, and try again. Learning from a cheap failure on the bench is far better than learning from an expensive one on site.

Key ideas

Expect failures. The Skills for Success description of creativity and innovation says to "expect failures" and "learn from failures to improve." A failed test is information, not a bad mark.

Predict, test, record. Before a test, write what you think will happen. During the test, watch closely. After it, write down what actually happened, in plain words.

Observation and guess are different. "The arm folded at the table edge at 9 washers" is an observation. "The cardboard was too weak" is a guess about why. Write the observation first, then your guess, then how you would check the guess.

Change one thing at a time. If you change three things between versions and the bracket holds more, you do not know which change helped. This is called a fair test.

Reverse the idea. If pushing up from underneath does not work, what if something pulls from above?

Averages. Test each version more than once and take the average. Using BEDMAS, add first because of the brackets, then divide:

average = (trial 1 + trial 2 + trial 3) ÷ 3

Percent improvement = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

The test

Your team builds a bracket from one piece of corrugated cardboard (about 30 cm by 40 cm) and 1 m of masking tape. Tape the base of the bracket to the top of the table. The arm must reach 15 cm past the table edge. Hang a paper cup from the end of the arm. Add washers 2 at a time until the bracket fails. The goal is to hold 20 washers for 10 seconds.

Safety

If you cut cardboard with a utility knife, follow these steps from the Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety:

  • cut on a stable surface, such as a cutting mat
  • wear safety glasses, which protect your eyes if the blade breaks
  • cut away from your body, and keep your other hand and your body out of the path the blade could take if it slips
  • do not use too much pressure when cutting
  • retract the blade fully right after each cut
  • do not try to catch a falling knife. Step back and let it fall
  • put dull or broken blades in a puncture-resistant container

Scissors are a good choice for most cuts. Close them when you put them down.

During the load test, keep the load small and keep your feet and hands out from under the cup. Put a box on the floor under the cup to catch the washers.

Worked example

Marcus's team builds version 1: a flat strip of cardboard taped to the table, sticking out 15 cm. They predict 20 washers. Three trials: 8, 10 and 9 washers. Each time, the arm folds down at the table edge.

Average for version 1: (8 + 10 + 9) ÷ 3 = 27 ÷ 3 = 9 washers.

Observation: the arm bends at the table edge. Guess: a flat strip bends easily. Check: make the arm stiffer and see if the bend moves or stops.

Version 2, one change: they fold the arm into a triangle-shaped tube. Trials: 14, 16 and 15.

Average for version 2: (14 + 16 + 15) ÷ 3 = 45 ÷ 3 = 15 washers.

Percent improvement: (15 − 9) ÷ 9 × 100 = 6 ÷ 9 × 100 ≈ 66.7%

Version 2 still fails, now because the tape peels off the table. For version 3 they reverse the idea: instead of a brace pushing up from below, they run a strip of cardboard from the end of the arm back to a taped spot on the table, so it pulls from above.

Task 5 (level 2) Change one thing only. Build version 2 and test it three times. Record it in the table. If you have time, build version 3. Try the reverse prompt for at least one version.

Task 6 (level 3) Another team tested two versions.

  • Version 1: 6, 7 and 8 washers
  • Version 2: 12, 13 and 14 washers

a) Find the average for each version. Show your work using BEDMAS.

b) Find the percent improvement from version 1 to version 2. Round to one decimal place.

c) Now do the same for your own team's best two versions.

Task 7 (level 2) Write a short test report of 4 to 6 sentences. Say what failed and how, what you changed and why, what you learned, and what you would change next. Then share one tip from your test with another team.

Task 8 (level 3) A pipefitting crew at Coldwater Mechanical (a fictional company) bench-tests two designs for a temporary pipe support on a test rig. Each design is loaded until it slips.

  • Design A: 180, 175 and 185 kg
  • Design B, with one change (a second U-bolt): 260, 250 and 270 kg

a) Find the average for each design. Show your work using BEDMAS.

b) Find the percent improvement from A to B. Round to one decimal place.

c) Was this a fair test? What one thing would you check?

d) Why can a bench test like this not decide what goes into a real building?

Check yourself

  1. Why should you change only one thing between versions?
  2. Which of these is an observation and which is a guess? "The tape let go at 14 washers." "The tape was the wrong kind."
  3. Three trials held 10, 11 and 15 washers. What is the average?
  4. Name three safety steps for cutting cardboard with a utility knife.
  5. Why is a failed test still useful?
