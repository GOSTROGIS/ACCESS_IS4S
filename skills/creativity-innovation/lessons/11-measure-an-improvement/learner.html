Measure an improvement

Run a fair test of an old way and a new way, work out the average, percent time saved and break-even, and decide if the change is worth it.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Apply your ideas; Develop your ideas

Why this matters on the job

"It feels faster" does not convince a foreman. Numbers do. In lesson 7 you pitched an idea with a number. This lesson shows you how to get good numbers: time the old way and the new way fairly, check the quality as well as the speed, and work out whether the change pays for itself. That is how a good idea becomes the way the crew works.

Key ideas

Fair test. Compare like with like. Change only the method (lesson 6). Keep the same:

  • person, or people with the same experience
  • material and size of piece
  • tool and set-up, apart from the change you are testing
  • way of timing, from the same start point to the same end point

Do several trials of each, not one. Record every trial. Do not drop a slow or bad one unless you write down a real reason, such as "the saw jammed".

Average. Add the trials, then divide by how many. Brackets first (BEDMAS):

average = (trial 1 + trial 2 + ... + last trial) ÷ number of trials

Range. The largest trial minus the smallest. A small range means the method gives the same result every time.

Percent time saved. When the new way is faster, the time goes down:

percent time saved = (old time − new time) ÷ old time × 100

Always divide by the old time.

Quality counts. Check the result as well as the time. A faster cut that is the wrong length is not an improvement. Decide your check before the test, for example "within 1 mm of the target length".

Break-even. If the change takes set-up time:

break-even = set-up time ÷ time saved per piece

Past the break-even point, every piece is a saving.

Worked example

Ridgeline Framing Ltd. (a fictional company) needs 240 pieces of blocking cut to 368 mm. Priya, a second-year apprentice, thinks a stop block clamped to the mitre saw fence would be faster than measuring each piece with a tape. Setting up the stop block takes 10 minutes.

Plan a fair test. Priya cuts all 10 pieces herself, from the same stack of 2×4, on the same saw. She times each cut from picking up the board to setting down the cut piece. She measures each piece to the nearest millimetre. Her quality check: within 1 mm of 368 mm.

Record.

Trial 1 2 3 4 5
Old way, seconds 52 47 55 49 52
New way, seconds 21 18 20 19 22

Averages.

  • old: (52 + 47 + 55 + 49 + 52) ÷ 5 = 255 ÷ 5 = 51 seconds
  • new: (21 + 18 + 20 + 19 + 22) ÷ 5 = 100 ÷ 5 = 20 seconds

Range. Old: 55 − 47 = 8 seconds. New: 22 − 18 = 4 seconds. The new way is also more even.

Percent time saved. (51 − 20) ÷ 51 × 100 = 31 ÷ 51 × 100 = 60.8%, rounded to one decimal place.

Quality.

Old way: 366, 371, 368, 365, 370 mm. Only 1 of 5 is within 1 mm. New way: 368, 368, 369, 368, 367 mm. All 5 are within 1 mm.

Scale up to the job.

  • time saved per piece: 51 − 20 = 31 seconds
  • on 240 pieces: 31 × 240 = 7440 seconds = 7440 ÷ 60 = 124 minutes
  • minus the 10 minutes of set-up: 124 − 10 = 114 minutes saved, almost 2 hours

Break-even. Set-up is 10 minutes = 600 seconds. 600 ÷ 31 = 19.4. After 20 pieces, the stop block has paid for itself.

Decide. Faster by about 61%, more accurate, and paid back after 20 of 240 pieces. Priya pitches it with these numbers, and the stop block becomes the crew's normal method.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Use Priya's bar chart. Which old-way cut was fastest? Which new-way cut was slowest?

Task 2 (level 2) A welding apprentice times tack-welding brackets with and without a fixture. Without: 95, 88, 102, 91, 94 seconds. With: 41, 38, 44, 40, 37 seconds.

a) Find each average. Use BEDMAS and show your work.

b) Find the range of each.

Task 3 (level 2) Use your averages from Task 2. Find the percent time saved. Round to one decimal place.

Task 4 (level 2) Target length 610 mm, quality check ±2 mm. Old way: 607, 612, 610, 615, 609 mm. New way: 610, 611, 609, 610, 612 mm. How many cuts pass the check with each method?

Task 5 (level 2) Use Task 2. The shop has 150 brackets to tack.

a) How many seconds does the fixture save on the whole job? How many minutes?

b) The fixture takes 45 minutes to build. What is the net time saved?

Task 6 (level 2) A stop block takes 8 minutes to set up and saves 24 seconds a piece. After how many pieces does it pay for itself? Round up to a whole piece.

Task 7 (level 3) Jordan tested a new way to cut drywall. Find three things that make his test unfair, and say how to fix each one.

  • Jordan cut 5 sheets the old way on Monday morning. His helper cut 5 sheets the new way on Friday afternoon.
  • The old-way sheets were 1/2 inch drywall. The new-way sheets were 5/8 inch.
  • Jordan dropped the slowest new-way time "because it was a bad one".
  • He timed the old way from when he picked up the sheet, and the new way from when he started the first cut.

Task 8 (level 3) A sprinkler fitter cuts 400 pieces of strut channel. The old way, with a hacksaw, takes 90 seconds a cut. The new way, with a portable band saw the shop already owns and a stop jig, takes 35 seconds a cut. The jig costs $60 in material and takes 1 hour to make. For this task, use a labour cost of $45 an hour.

a) How many hours does the new way save on the 400 cuts? Round to two decimal places.

b) What is that time worth in dollars?

c) What does the jig cost in total, material and labour?

d) Is the jig worth it? Give the net saving.

Task 9 (level 3) A new method for cutting pipe nipples averages 15 seconds a cut instead of 28. But 2 of 5 test cuts were 3 mm too long. The job allows ±1 mm.

a) Find the percent time saved.

b) Should the crew switch? Explain using speed and quality.

c) What would you test next?

Task 10 (level 2) Write a short result statement (3 to 4 sentences) for Priya to give her foreman. Include the average times, the percent time saved, the quality result and the saving on the whole job.

Check yourself

  1. Name three things you keep the same in a fair test.
  2. Why do you divide by the old time when you work out percent time saved?
  3. Old average 40 seconds, new average 30 seconds. What is the percent time saved?
  4. What does break-even tell you?
  5. Why is a faster method not always an improvement?
