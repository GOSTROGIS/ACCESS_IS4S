Pitching an improvement to a supervisor

Present a tested idea to a supervisor in two minutes, answer their questions, and take their ideas on board.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 75 min
  • Components: Apply your ideas; Facilitate a creative and innovative environment for yourself and others; Use your imagination and curiosity

Why this matters on the job

A good idea that stays in your head does not change anything. On a crew, ideas go through the foreman, the lead hand or the superintendent. They are busy, and they have to think about cost, safety, schedule and the client. A short, clear pitch with numbers and a small test is much more likely to get a yes than "we should do it differently." Being open to the supervisor's questions, and to their ideas, often makes your idea better.

Key ideas

Pick the right moment. Ask for two minutes at a break or at the end of the day, not in the middle of a concrete pour.

A two-minute pitch has six parts:

  1. Problem: what you saw, with a number ("Each of us walks to the sea-can about 6 times a day.")
  2. Idea: what you want to try, in one or two sentences
  3. Test: what you already tried and what happened, including what did not work
  4. Cost and time: what it would take, and what it would save
  5. Safety: how it keeps or improves safety, and how it follows the site rules
  6. Ask: a small, clear request ("Can we try it for one week on Building C?")

Listen to the answer. The supervisor's questions are not an attack. They are checking things you may not have thought of. Answer what you can. If you do not know, say "I don't know yet. I'll find out."

Be open to their ideas. The Skills for Success model describes being "open to new ideas without judging." If the supervisor suggests a change, try it on before you defend your version.

"No" or "not yet" is not the end. Ask, "What would you need to see to try it?" Then go and get that.

Give credit. If others helped with the idea or the test, name them.

Safety ideas have a second path. If your idea is about safety, you can also bring it to the joint health and safety committee or, on a smaller site, the worker health and safety representative. In BC, a workplace with 20 or more workers needs a joint committee. One with 10 to 19 workers needs a worker representative. Part of their job is to deal with workers' health and safety concerns and make recommendations.

Worked example

Jordan is a first-year apprentice at Ridgeline Framing Ltd. (a fictional company). At the end of the day he asks his foreman, Dana, for two minutes.

"Our crew walks to the sea-can for fasteners about 6 times each a day. I timed it at about 4 minutes a round trip, which is about 96 minutes a day for the four of us. I'd like to try a rolling gang box on the deck near where we're working. Yesterday Priya and I moved the small parts bin to the deck for the afternoon, and we only made 2 trips each. A rolling box from the yard would cost nothing, and we'd save about an hour a day. It stays out of the walkways, and I'd roll it back and lock it in the sea-can at the end of every day, so the site lock-up rule is still met. Could we try it for one week on Building C?"

Dana asks: "Who's responsible if it gets left out overnight?" Jordan: "Me. I'll do the last trip at 3:30." Dana adds: "Put it on the second floor, not the first. That's where we'll be all week." Jordan says, "Good idea. That cuts more trips," and thanks Priya for helping with the test.

Practice

Task 1 (level 2) Pick an idea to pitch. Use one from an earlier lesson (the trips in lesson 2, the marking jig in lesson 3, your bracket in lesson 6) or one of your own from a job you know. Fill in the pitch planner.

Idea
Test (what I tried, what happened)
Cost and time
Safety
Ask

Task 2 (level 2) Work out the saving for your pitch. Show your work using BEDMAS. If your idea saves time, give it in minutes a day and hours a week. If it saves material or money, show that instead.

Task 3 (level 2) Role play. Give your pitch to a partner in 2 minutes or less. Your partner plays the supervisor, using one of the supervisor cards below.

Task 4 (level 2) When you play the supervisor, read your card, then listen to the whole pitch without interrupting. Ask two questions from your card or your own. Offer one idea that could make the pitch better.

Supervisor card A, the busy foreman. You like ideas but the schedule is tight. Ask: "How long will it take to set up?" and "What happens if it doesn't work?"

Supervisor card B, the careful superintendent. Safety and site rules come first. Ask: "How does this fit the site rules?" and "Who checks it every day?"

Supervisor card C, the "we've always done it this way" lead hand. You have done this for 20 years. Ask: "What's wrong with the way we do it now?" and "Who else has tried this?"

Task 5 (level 3) Your supervisor says, "Not yet. Show me it works first." Write what you will do next and what you will bring back to them.

Task 6 (level 2) Write one idea from your supervisor or partner that you will add to your pitch, and how it makes your idea better.

Check yourself

  1. Name the six parts of a two-minute pitch.
  2. Why is "Can we try it for one week?" a better ask than "Can we change how we do this?"
  3. A supervisor asks a question you cannot answer. What do you say?
  4. Your idea is about a safety concern. Besides your supervisor, who else could you bring it to on a BC workplace with 25 workers?
