Spotting a better way on the job site

Look hard at a routine job-site task, question the habits behind it and find where a change could help.

  • Learner
  • Instructor
  • Answers
  • Level: 1-2
  • Time: 60 min
  • Components: Identify opportunities for you to innovate; Use your imagination and curiosity

Why this matters on the job

Every crew has routines: where the gang box sits, who gets the fasteners, how material is staged. Some routines are there for a good reason, like site security or safety. Others are just habits nobody has checked in years. A worker who can spot wasted walking, waiting or repeating, and say so clearly, saves the crew time and money. Supervisors notice the people who see a better way.

Key ideas

A norm or habit is "the way we always do it." It is not wrong. It just has not been questioned.

Waste you can see on a site usually looks like one of three things:

  • walking: trips back and forth for tools, fasteners or drawings
  • waiting: standing by for a lift, a key, a decision or another trade
  • repeating: measuring or setting up the same thing over and over

Real limits and possibly artificial limits. A real limit must be kept: a safety rule, a code requirement, a site rule from the prime contractor, the client's drawings. A possibly artificial limit is a rule nobody has checked. It might be there for a reason you do not know yet, so you ask before you change it.

Never treat a safety rule as artificial. If you think a safety rule makes a job slower, bring it to your supervisor. On a larger BC workplace you can also bring it to the joint health and safety committee or the worker health and safety representative. Do not work around it.

An opportunity statement names the problem and who it affects, without giving the answer yet. Start it with "How might we …". For example: "How might we cut the number of trips framers make for fasteners?" This leaves room for many ideas. "How might we buy a rolling gang box?" already picks one answer.

Worked example

Ridgeline Framing Ltd. (a fictional company) is framing townhouses on a Fraser Valley site. The gang box with fasteners and small tools lives in a locked sea-can at the far end of the site. Each of the 4 framers walks to it about 6 times a day. A round trip takes about 4 minutes.

Time spent walking for one day, using BEDMAS (no brackets or exponents here, so multiply left to right):

4 × 6 × 4 = 96 minutes a day

For a 5-day week: 96 × 5 = 480 minutes, which is 480 ÷ 60 = 8 hours. That is a full day of one person's work, every week, spent walking.

Sorting the limits:

Limit Real or possibly artificial? Why
Tools must be locked up at the end of the day Real Site security rule from the prime contractor
The gang box stays in the sea-can all day Possibly artificial Nobody remembers why. Ask the foreman
Only the lead hand hands out fasteners Possibly artificial Started years ago on a different job

Opportunity statement: "How might we get fasteners and small tools closer to the framers during the day, and still lock everything up at night?"

If a rolling gang box near the work cut trips to 2 per framer, the time saved each day would be, brackets first:

(6 − 2) × 4 × 4 = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 minutes a day

Practice

Task 2 (level 1) Read the Ridgeline Framing example again. List two habits on that site that could be questioned.

Task 3 (level 2) Here are limits on a fictional pipe-trades job. Coldwater Mechanical is running copper on the fourth floor of a new clinic. Mark each one Real or Possibly artificial, and say who you would ask about the ones you are not sure of.

Limit Real or possibly artificial? Who to ask
The gang box stays on the ground floor
Hot work needs a permit from the prime contractor
Fittings are ordered one day at a time
Only the foreman can carry the drawings
Workers wear fall protection near the open slab edge

Task 4 (level 2) On the Coldwater job, 3 pipefitters each make 5 trips a day to the gang box on the ground floor. A round trip takes 6 minutes. Show your work using BEDMAS.

a) How many minutes a day does the crew spend on these trips? __

b) How many hours is that in a 5-day week? __

c) If a box on the fourth floor cut trips to 2 per person, how many minutes a day would the crew save? __

Task 5 (level 2) Write an opportunity statement for the Coldwater job, or for the routine you mapped in Task 1. Start with "How might we …". Do not put a solution in it. Include one real limit that must be kept.

Check yourself

  1. What are the three kinds of waste you can see on a site?
  2. Give one example of a limit that is real and one that might be artificial.
  3. Why should you never treat a safety rule as an artificial limit?
  4. A crew of 2 makes 8 trips each a day. Each trip takes 5 minutes. A change cuts trips to 3 each. How many minutes a day are saved? Use BEDMAS.
  5. What is wrong with this opportunity statement: "How might we buy a second sea-can?"
