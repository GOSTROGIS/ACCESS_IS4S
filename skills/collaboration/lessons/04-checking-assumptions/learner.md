# Checking assumptions before you act

## Why this matters on the job

A tool goes missing and someone says, "The new guy had it last." A lockout tag is on the panel, so everyone assumes the power is off. A quiet apprentice is passed over for a task because "she probably can't handle it." Each time, someone filled a gap with a guess. Some guesses are only unfair. Some are dangerous. Checking before you act protects people and keeps a crew's trust.

## Key ideas

**What you saw and what you think are different.** You *saw* the laser level is not in the gang box. You *think* the apprentice took it home. The first is an observation. The second is an assumption.

**An assumption** fills a gap in what we know. We all make them. They save time, and many are harmless. They become a problem when we act on them as if they were facts.

**A bias** is a leaning we already have about a person, a group or a situation, before we have the facts. It comes from our past experience, our family, our friends, the media and the culture around us. Often we don't notice it. Biases can be unfavourable ("people from the city can't work outdoors") or favourable ("he's from my hometown, he'll be a good worker"). Both can lead us wrong.

**A stereotype** is a belief about a group that we apply to every person in that group. It stops us from seeing the person in front of us.

**Four steps before you act on a guess:**

1. **Notice** that you are making a judgment.
2. **Separate** what you saw or heard from what you think it means.
3. **Check:** ask, look, read the sign-out sheet, test it.
4. **Decide** based on what you found.

### Worked example: the missing laser level

Harbour View Builders (a fictional company). At 7:00 the laser level is not in the gang box. Kyle says, "The new apprentice had it last. Bet he took it home."

| What Kyle saw or heard | What Kyle thinks it means |
|---|---|
| The laser level is not in the gang box | It is missing |
| The new apprentice used it yesterday | He took it home |

**Check:** Kyle reads the sign-out sheet. It says the lead signed the laser out at 3:30 yesterday. The laser is in the second gang box on level 2, where the lead used it last.

If Kyle had told the crew "the new guy took it," the apprentice would have been blamed for something he didn't do. One question would have saved that.

## Practice

**Task 1** (level 1)
Read the story once. Then mark each statement **T** (true), **F** (false) or **?** (the story does not tell you). Do not reread until you have marked them all.

> At 6:40 a.m. a white pickup pulled into the Harbour View site. A person in a hard hat and high-vis vest walked to the site trailer, opened the lock box and took out a key. A few minutes later the scissor lift was running. When the site superintendent arrived at 7:05, the lift was parked by the east wall and the trailer door was open. A note on the lift's controls said "Battery low."

**Task 2** (level 1)
Mark each line **O** (observation: something you can see, hear or measure) or **A** (assumption).

a) The truck is parked in the fire lane.
b) He doesn't care about the rules.
c) She has asked three questions this morning.
d) She isn't ready for this job.
e) The concrete delivery was 40 minutes late.
f) The supplier is always unreliable.
g) He hasn't said anything at the crew talk.
h) He has nothing to add.

**Task 3** (level 2)
Your instructor will read some statements. Move to the sign that fits what you think: Agree strongly, Agree a little, Disagree a little, Disagree strongly. Stand in the middle if you can't decide. Then, in groups of four, talk about these questions and have one person take notes:

- Where do beliefs like these come from? (family, media, past experience, friends)
- Why do people hold on to them?
- What harm can they do on a crew?
- How does it feel when someone judges you before they know you?

## Check yourself

1. What is the difference between an observation and an assumption?
2. What is a stereotype?
3. Can a bias be favourable? Give an example.
4. Name three places biases come from.
5. A tool is missing. Before you blame someone, name two things you could check.
