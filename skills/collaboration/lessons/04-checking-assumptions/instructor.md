# Checking assumptions before you act

**Collaboration lesson 4 of 15 · 100 min · Level 1-3**

Every minute of the session is in the Learning tasks table. The blocks below point to their step numbers.

## Opening (6 min, step 1.1)

Welcome learners to session 4 of 15. This session runs 100 minutes.

Hook, "Choose someone" (adapted from the ACCESS biases lesson, with written descriptions in place of the magazine collage). Read these four co-workers aloud and write them on the board:

- A: 22 years old, skateboards to work
- B: 61 years old, grey hair, reading glasses
- C: wears long painted nails and a lot of jewellery off the job
- D: shows up to the interview in a suit

Ask: "Who would you pick to help you with a heavy lift? Who would you ask to fix your phone? Who would you want as your crew lead?" Learners choose silently, then tell a partner how they chose. Ask the group: "What did you actually know about these people? What did you fill in?"

## Objective (2 min, step 1.2)

By the end of the session, learners will be able to:

- tell an observation from an assumption in a short job-site story (Value diversity and inclusivity of others: respond without judging)
- explain bias and stereotype in plain words, and where biases come from (Value diversity and inclusivity of others: acknowledge and accept differences among people)
- use four steps to check a guess before acting on it (Work well with other people: engage in trust building behaviours)
- name one bias of their own and a way to check it

## Pre-assessment (4 min, step 1.3)

1. What is an assumption? Give an example from work or daily life.
2. Have you ever been judged by someone before they knew you? How did it affect how you worked with them?

### Task 3 statements

Read these one at a time. They are common job-site beliefs, chosen so learners can examine them. Move on quickly.

1. The person who talks most at a meeting knows the most.
2. Older workers have a hard time learning new technology.
3. Someone who asks a lot of questions isn't ready for the job.
4. Women aren't strong enough for the heavy trades.
5. A worker with a lot of tattoos is less professional.
6. People from the city don't know how to work outdoors.
7. Young workers don't want to work hard.
8. If someone doesn't look you in the eye, they're hiding something.

## Post-assessment (4 min, step 3.1)

1. In the Harbour View story, what is one thing that felt true but was not in the story?
2. What are the four steps before you act on a guess?
3. Give one example of how a bias could cause a safety problem on a job site.

## Summary (2 min, step 3.2)

During this session we covered:

- the difference between an observation and an assumption
- bias, favourable and unfavourable, and where it comes from
- stereotypes, and how they hide the person in front of us
- four steps before acting on a guess: notice, separate, check, decide
- a private plan to check one of our own biases

Next session: preventing and resolving conflict on a crew.

## Notes for the instructor

- This lesson rebuilds the ACCESS biases, assumptions and stereotypes lesson and the Restaurant exercise. The Restaurant story and the store robbery story inside the ACCESS lesson come from a published exercise and are not used. The Harbour View story and its statements are new.
- The ACCESS list of social biases for Four Corners is replaced with job-site statements. Some of the old statements named racial groups. Do not use them.
- In Task 3, some learners may hold a view that hurts others in the room. Keep the activity moving, and bring the group back to the questions in step 2.6 rather than arguing with one person.
- Statement 8 can be a cultural difference: in some families or communities, avoiding direct eye contact with an older person or a boss is a sign of respect. Let learners raise this from their own experience rather than presenting it as true of a whole group.
