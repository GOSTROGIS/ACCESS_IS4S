# Run a pre-task huddle and hand over at shift end

## Why this matters on the job

Many incidents start with "I thought you knew." A five-minute huddle before a task puts everyone on the same plan. A clear handover at the end of a shift means the next crew does not walk into an open floor hole, a half-finished anchor or a tool that is not where they think. These two short talks are where collaboration happens every day on a site.

## Key ideas

**A pre-task huddle** is a short talk at the work area, right before a task starts. It is not the same as a toolbox talk. A toolbox talk teaches a topic. A huddle plans one task, today, with this crew. Your site may have its own form and its own name for it. Use theirs.

1. **The task.** What we are doing, where and by when.
2. **The hazards.** What could hurt someone here today. Ask the crew. The newest person often sees something the others stopped noticing.
3. **The controls.** What we will do about each hazard.
4. **Who does what.** A name for each job. Nobody is left watching.
5. **Questions.** Ask for questions. Remind everyone that anyone who sees something unsafe stops and reports it. In BC, a person who sees an unsafe condition must report it as soon as possible to a supervisor or the employer.

**Make it a conversation.** Ask open questions: "What else could go wrong here?" "Who hasn't done this before?" Let people answer. A huddle where only the lead talks is a speech.

**A shift handover** passes the work to the next person or crew. Do it face to face if you can, and in writing every time. The handover card covers six things:

| Part | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Done** | What is finished, with a count or location. |
| **Not done** | What is left, and where. |
| **Hazards** | Anything unsafe or changed: openings, live circuits, lockouts, wet concrete. |
| **Tools and gear** | Where things are, and their state (charged, tagged out). |
| **Watch for** | Anything the next crew must know before starting. |
| **Questions to** | Who to call, with a number. |

**Write numbers and places exactly.** "Most of the anchors" is not a handover. "14 of 20 anchors, grid C to D left" is.

### Worked example: the anchor job

Harbourside Concrete Anchors (fictional) is drilling anchors for a steel ledger on level 2. Sam, the day-shift lead hand, runs this huddle at 7:00:

> **Task:** "We're drilling 20 anchors on the east wall of level 2, grid A to D, done by 3:00."
>
> **Hazards:** "What could hurt us?" The crew names the floor opening at C4, silica dust from drilling, and rebar in the wall.
>
> **Controls:** "The opening stays covered and tagged. We use the hammer with the dust extractor. We scan before each hole, and if we hit rebar, we stop and call me."
>
> **Who does what:** "Kai drills. Rose scans and marks. Ben runs the vacuum and moves the lift."
>
> **Questions:** "Questions? Anyone not done this before? Remember, anyone can stop the job."

At 3:00 the crew has drilled 14 holes. At C3 the scanner found rebar. The engineer said to move that hole 50 mm. Sam fills in the handover card shown above and walks the evening lead hand along the wall.

The evening crew knows exactly how many holes are left, where the opening is, where the hammer is and what to do at C3. Nobody has to call Sam at home.
