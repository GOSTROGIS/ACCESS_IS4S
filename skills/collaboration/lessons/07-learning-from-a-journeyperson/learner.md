# Learning from a journeyperson

## Why this matters on the job

In a BC apprenticeship, most of your learning happens on the job, beside a certified journeyperson. How well you learn depends a lot on that working relationship. An apprentice who asks clear questions, listens to feedback and shows progress gets taught more, and gets trusted with more. Later, you will be the one showing someone new.

## Key ideas

**How apprenticeship works in BC.** SkilledTradesBC runs the system. These points come from its website:

- About **80%** of your training happens at work, under the supervision of a certified journeyperson. About **20%** is in-class technical training with a SkilledTradesBC-designated training provider.
- Most apprenticeships take four or more years. The average across all trades is about 4 years.
- Your **sponsor** (often your employer) makes sure you get on-the-job training and mentorship from a certified journeyperson. You should know which journeyperson is responsible for supervising you.
- Your sponsor reports your **work-based training hours** in the SkilledTradesBC Portal. SkilledTradesBC recommends every 3 to 6 months. One hour on the job counts as one hour of work-based training.
- Sign in to your own Portal account often to check that your hours are there. If they are missing or wrong, contact your sponsor.
- At the end, your sponsor and the journeyperson who supervised you give a **Recommendation for Certification**, confirming you work at a journeyperson's level.

**Be easy to teach.**

- Watch first. Ask to see it done once.
- Ask at the right time. Don't interrupt someone in the middle of a cut or a lift, unless it is about safety. Never guess on safety: stop and ask.
- Repeat back what you were shown: "So first I check..., then I..."
- Write it down. A small notebook in your pocket beats asking the same thing three times.
- Try it while they watch, then ask, "What should I change?"

**Ask clear questions.** "How do I do this?" is hard to answer. "I've measured twice and I get 2 mm different each time. Am I hooking the tape wrong?" shows what you tried and where you are stuck.

**Take feedback well.**

1. Listen to the end. Don't start explaining.
2. Say thanks, even if it stings.
3. Ask a question to understand: "Can you show me where it goes wrong?"
4. Do it differently next time.
5. Follow up: "Is this better?"

The five steps for taking feedback make a loop. Step 5 brings you back to listening.

**Mentoring goes both ways.** The Skills for Success model includes supporting others through coaching, mentoring and motivating. Once you can do a task well, you can show the next new person. Teaching something is also one of the best ways to check that you really know it.

### Worked example: cuts running long

Tidewater Carpentry (a fictional company). Journeyperson Bev tells apprentice Eli: "Your stud cuts are coming out about 3 mm long."

**Response A:** "That's not on me. The saw's off."
Eli defends himself before he knows the cause. Bev is less likely to spend time teaching him.

**Response B:** "Okay, thanks. Can you watch me do the next one and tell me where it's going wrong?"
Bev watches and sees Eli is lining up the blade on the wrong side of his pencil line. She shows him once. Eli's next three cuts are right, and he writes the fix in his notebook. At the end of the day he says, "Checked the last ten, all on the mark."

**Task 1** (level 1)
Is this a good time to ask your journeyperson a question? Write **now**, **wait** or **stop and ask right away**.

a) They are on a ladder in the middle of a cut.
b) You are about to start a task you've never done.
c) The crane operator is taking signals from your journeyperson.
d) It's coffee break, and you have a bigger question about your training plan.
e) You are not sure the power to the circuit you are about to touch is off.
f) They have just shown you a step, and you didn't catch part of it.

**Task 2** (level 1)
Make each question clear and specific. Add what you tried or what you see.

a) "How do I do this?"
