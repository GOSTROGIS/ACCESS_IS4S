# Disagree with a lead hand and still get the work done

## Why this matters on the job

You will not agree with every call your lead hand makes. Sometimes you will be right. Sometimes you will not know what they know: the schedule, the engineer's note, the budget, the client. Workers who argue every call, or who go quiet and do sloppy work, lose trust fast. Workers who speak up once, clearly, then do the job well, earn the right to be listened to. And when it is about safety, the rules are different.

## Key ideas

**First question: is it a safety issue?**

- **Yes.** You do not have to argue or "disagree and commit." In BC, if you see an unsafe condition, you must report it as soon as possible to your supervisor or employer (OHS Regulation section 3.10). If you have reasonable cause to believe a task would create an undue hazard, you must not do it, and you must report it right away (section 3.12). Communication lesson 4 teaches the refusal steps.
- **No.** It is a work disagreement: a method, an order of tasks, a tool, a layout. Use the steps below.

**Steps for a work disagreement:**

1. **Pick the moment.** Not in the middle of a lift or a pour. Not in front of the crew if you can avoid it.
2. **Say it once, with facts.** Use W.I.N. from lesson 5: when (what you see), I (the effect on the work), need (your idea). "When we run the conduit before the sprinkler lines, I think we'll have to move it twice. Can we wait a day for the sprinkler fitters?"
3. **Ask, then listen.** "Is there a reason we're doing it this way?" There may be one you do not know.
4. **The lead hand decides.** It is their call and their responsibility.
5. **Disagree and commit.** If it is not a safety issue, do it their way, and do it well. No slow work, no "I told you so," no complaining to the crew.
6. **If the problem keeps happening,** bring facts to the foreman, in private. "Three times this week we've moved conduit after the sprinkler crew came through. Here are the photos."

**Commit means really commit.** Doing a job badly to prove a point damages the work, the crew and your name. If your way was better, the results will show it over time.

**Going up the chain.** Most problems are solved at the lowest step. Go up one step at a time, with facts. Safety concerns that are not fixed can go to your health and safety representative or joint health and safety committee if your workplace has one. If unsafe work is still not resolved after the refusal steps, WorkSafeBC investigates.

**Your tone matters as much as your point.** Lessons 3 and 5 of the Communication series cover assertive statements and taking criticism. Stay calm and factual. Do not make it personal: "Your plan won't work" invites a fight. "I see a problem with the order. Can I show you?" invites a look.

### Worked example: the backfill

Cedar Ridge Civil (fictional) is laying a storm drain. Ravi, a second-year pipelayer apprentice, thinks the crew should compact the backfill in thinner layers than the lead hand, Bo, has asked for.

**Is it a safety issue?** Not directly. Nobody is in danger from the layer thickness itself. It is a work quality question.

**Ravi waits for a pause,** then speaks to Bo away from the excavator:

> "Bo, can I ask about the backfill? When we lift it in 300 mm layers, I'm not sure the plate tamper gets it all. I need to know we'll pass the compaction test. Could we try 150?"

**Bo explains:** "Good catch to ask. The engineer's spec for this job says 300 mm lifts with the big roller, not the plate. The roller's coming at 10:00."

**Ravi commits:** "Got it. 300 with the roller. I'll stage the next load."

Ravi was not wrong to ask. Bo knew something Ravi did not. Ravi spoke once, listened, and got back to work. Next time Bo is more likely to listen to Ravi.

**If Bo had said "Just do it" with no reason,** Ravi would still do it Bo's way, and well. If the compaction test failed later, Ravi would bring the facts to the foreman calmly, not to the crew.
