Preventing and resolving conflict on a crew

Spot conflict early, listen so the other person feels heard, and say what you need in a way that solves the problem.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 100 min
  • Components: Manage difficult interactions with other people; Work well with other people

Why this matters on the job

Two apprentices need the same tool. A lead and a journeyperson disagree on the order of work. A sub keeps leaving a mess in your area. Conflict happens any time people work together. Left alone, it slows the job, people stop talking to each other, and safety slips. Handled well, it solves a real problem and the crew works better afterward.

Key ideas

Where conflict comes from. Most conflicts come from differences in:

  • opinions: "We should frame the east wall first."
  • facts: "The drawing says 16-inch centres." "No, it says 24."
  • perceptions: how each person sees what happened
  • values: what each person thinks matters most, such as speed or quality

Conflict also starts when people are not listening, or when they are not working from the same information.

Warning signs. Watch for:

  • sharp or sarcastic comments
  • people getting impatient
  • ideas shot down before the person finishes talking
  • the same point repeated over and over
  • people taking sides
  • lots of talk but no decision
  • finger pointing, and a lot of "you should"

Prevent it. Many conflicts never happen when the crew shares the same plan and drawings, checks in at the start of the day, knows who makes which decisions, and raises small problems while they are still small.

How people respond to conflict. Most of us have a habit. We might put it off, give in, push until we win, meet halfway, or work it out together. No habit is always right. If the issue is small, letting it go can be fine. If both people care about the result, working it out together usually lasts longest.

The chart places the five habits by how much you push for your own needs and how much you look after the other person's needs. Working it out together is high on both.

Four listening skills:

  1. Attend: stop what you're doing and pay attention to the other person.
  2. Value: show you respect them, even if you disagree with their idea.
  3. Paraphrase: say back what you heard, in your own words. "So you're saying..."
  4. Question: ask open questions. "What would work for you?" Be willing to change your mind.

Look for what makes sense in the other person's view before you push your own.

Say what you need: the W.I.N. method

  • When you... (describe what happened, not the person)
  • I... (say how it affects you or the work)
  • Need... (say what you want to happen)

Keep it natural, not like a script. With a boss, stick to facts and ask for time to talk. For example, instead of "I feel overwhelmed when you give me too much work," say: "I've stayed late four days this week to finish my work. Can we talk about my workload? When is a good time?"

Not every problem is a conflict to work out between two people. Threats, harassment and discrimination are not disagreements. You don't have to negotiate with them. Tell your supervisor. Lesson 8 covers this.

Worked example: one rotary hammer

Ridgeview Mechanical (a fictional company) has one rotary hammer on site. Apprentices Ana and Jordan both need it to drill anchor holes.

Jordan keeps picking it up while Ana is partway through a row.

How it went first: Ana: "You always grab it. You're so selfish." Jordan: "I've got work to do too. Get over it." They stop talking, and both rows fall behind.

Using W.I.N. and the listening skills: Ana: "When you take the hammer while I'm partway through a row, I lose my place and have to set up again. I need us to agree on turns. I'll be done my row by 10." Jordan (paraphrasing): "So you need it until 10 to finish the row, then I get it?" Ana: "Yes. What do you need?" Jordan: "I need it by 10 at the latest or I'll hold up the pipefitters." Ana: "Then 10 it is. I'll bring it to you."

The conflict was about the tool and the timing, not about who is selfish. Once each person said what they needed, the fix was easy.

Practice

Task 1 (level 2) Read this exchange from a fictional crew meeting. Underline every warning sign you find.

Lead: "We need to decide who's on the roof tomorrow." Mo: "Not me again. I'm always on the roof." Rita: "Oh, here we go." Mo: "I'm just saying, I'm always on the roof." Rita: "Well, you should have said something last week instead of..." Mo: "Don't tell me what I should have done." Lead: "Okay, let's..." Rita: "He does this every time."

Task 2 (level 2) My usual response. This is private. When I have a disagreement at work, I usually (tick all that apply):

  • [ ] put off dealing with it
  • [ ] try to meet the other person halfway
  • [ ] point to the rules or the boss to show I'm right
  • [ ] just give in
  • [ ] help the other person get what they want
  • [ ] keep pushing until I get my way
  • [ ] explain my side and ask for theirs
  • [ ] keep my ideas to myself
  • [ ] change the subject
  • [ ] try to understand the other person's side
  • [ ] get someone else to decide who's right
  • [ ] say I'm wrong even when I don't think I am

The kind of person I find easiest to work things out with is ___ because ___.

One thing I would like to change about how I handle conflict: ____

Task 3 (level 2) Paraphrase each statement. Start with "So you're saying..." or "So you need..."

a) "I've been on cleanup three days in a row and nobody else has done it once." b) "The drawings changed yesterday and nobody told the framers, so we built it wrong." c) "I don't mind starting early, but I need to be out by 3:30 to get my kid."

Task 4 (level 2) Write a W.I.N. statement for each situation.

a) A co-worker returns the crew van with the fuel light on, again. b) Your lead has given you all the cleanup for two weeks. (Remember: with a boss, stick to facts and ask for a time to talk.) c) A co-worker keeps talking over you at the morning toolbox talk.

Task 5 (level 2) "You've got it, I want it." Your instructor will give one partner an item from the box. Both partners want it badly for their work. Try to sort it out in 3 minutes, without force. Switch roles and try again. Then do it a third time using W.I.N. and the four listening skills.

What was different the third time? __________

Task 6 (level 2) Think of a conflict you are in now, or make one up. Write:

  1. What I want to happen: ____
  2. My W.I.N. statement: ____
  3. When and where I will say it: ____

Task 7 (level 2) Place it on the chart. A plumber and an electrician both need the same ceiling space for their runs. Name the habit from the chart for each response.

a) The plumber says, "Fine, take it. I'll figure something out." (He is annoyed.) b) The electrician says, "I was here first. My conduit goes in today." c) They agree the plumber goes this morning and the electrician gets the space after lunch. d) They look at the drawing together and find a route where both runs fit. e) Both leave it for tomorrow without talking.

Task 8 (level 3) You are the lead hand. Two of your apprentices, Kim and Raj (fictional), both need the only scissor lift first thing in the morning. They are arguing at the gang box.

a) Write what you say to start, using one of the four listening skills. b) Write one paraphrase you could use for Kim's side. c) Kim uses W.I.N. Write her sentence. d) Write the agreement the three of you reach. Say which habit from the chart it is.

Check yourself

  1. Name four things conflicts are usually about.
  2. Name three warning signs that a conflict is building.
  3. What does it mean to paraphrase? Why does it help?
  4. What do the letters in W.I.N. stand for?
  5. A co-worker threatens to hurt you if you report a safety problem. Is this a conflict to work out with W.I.N.? What should you do?
