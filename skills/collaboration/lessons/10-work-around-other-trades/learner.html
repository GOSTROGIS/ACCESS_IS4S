Work around other trades

Follow the order of trades on a shared job, know who waits on whom, respect inspection hold points and keep the next trade moving.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Achieve a common goal with others; Work well with other people; Manage difficult interactions with other people

Why this matters on the job

A washroom renovation can bring seven trades into one small room. The plumber cannot rough in until the framing is done. The drywaller cannot board until the inspector passes the pipes and wires behind the wall. The tiler cannot start until the board is up. If one trade is late, or covers something too soon, every trade after it loses time and money. Good trades know the order, know who is waiting on them and keep the next crew moving.

Key ideas

Trades work in a sequence. Some work must be finished before the next can start. That is called a dependency. "Rough-in" is the work inside the walls and floors: pipes, drains, vents, wires and boxes. "Finish" is what you see at the end: fixtures, cover plates, tile.

Hold points. In many BC cities, rough-in plumbing must be inspected and accepted before it is covered. Electrical rough wiring must be inspected, or the inspection waived by the safety authority, before any part of it is concealed. Many cities also inspect insulation and vapour barrier before drywall goes on. The permit and the inspection list for your job say which inspections apply. Until an inspection passes, nobody covers the work. That point in the job is a hold point.

Who waits on whom. The swimlane chart below shows a fictional 12-day washroom renovation. Each row is a trade. Read across for days. The arrows show who waits on whom.

Read the chart like this: framing is days 1 and 2. The plumber starts on day 3. The electrician overlaps the plumber on day 4. The rough-in inspection is day 6. Insulation, its inspection, drywall and tile follow.

Habits that keep the next trade moving:

  1. Know who is next. Ask your lead hand who follows you in this room.
  2. Say when you are done. Tell your foreman when an area is ready for the next trade or for inspection. Do not let people guess.
  3. Say early when you will be late. One day late for you can be one day late for every trade after you.
  4. Protect other trades' work. Do not step on pipes, bend boxes or lean material on fresh tape.
  5. Do not move another trade's material or tools without asking their crew or your foreman.
  6. Never cover work that has not passed inspection, even if you are told it "passed yesterday." Check with your foreman.

Problems between trades go foreman to foreman. If the drywallers keep boarding over your boxes, tell your foreman. Your foreman talks to their foreman or the superintendent. Arguing in the hallway with another company's crew rarely fixes the schedule.

Worked example: one day late

Use the swimlane chart. The electrician at Northline Electric (fictional) finds a damaged junction box on day 5. The rough-in now finishes at the end of day 6, not day 5.

  • The rough-in inspection cannot happen until the electrical rough-in is done. It moves from day 6 to day 7.
  • Insulation moves from day 7 to day 8. Its inspection moves from day 8 to day 9.
  • Drywall moves from days 9 and 10 to days 10 and 11.
  • Tile moves from days 11 and 12 to days 12 and 13.

One day late in electrical makes the whole job one day late. The plumber is not affected, because the plumber finished on day 4.

What the electrician's lead hand did right: at 10:00 on day 5 she told her foreman, "Damaged box in the washroom. Rough-in finishes end of day 6, not today. Can you tell the super so the inspection can move?" The super moved the inspection and called the insulator the same morning. Nobody showed up to a room they could not work in.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Rough-in or finish? Write R for rough-in or F for finish.

a) Setting the toilet _ b) Running the drain line in the floor c) Pulling wire to a switch box _ d) Putting cover plates on switches e) Grouting tile ____

Task 2 (level 2) Read the swimlane.

a) Which trades work on day 4? b) On what day is the rough-in inspection? c) Who is the insulator waiting on? d) How many days does the whole job take?

Task 3 (level 2) Hold point. Use the hold point figure.

a) What has to happen before anyone covers rough-in work? b) What happens if the inspection fails?

Task 4 (level 2) Who is affected? The plumber finishes rough-in one day late, at the end of day 5 instead of day 4. The electrician also finishes at the end of day 5, as planned. On what day can the rough-in inspection happen now? Does the finish date change? Explain.

Task 6 (level 2) The right words. Write the message you send your foreman when your area is ready for inspection.

Task 7 (level 2) Good habit or not? Write G for a good habit or N for not.

a) _ You lean your ladder on the plumber's new copper. b) You tell your foreman at 9:00 that you will be a day late. c) _ You move the tiler's buckets to get to your panel, without asking. d) You ask your lead hand who follows you in unit 3. e) ____ You board a wall because "the electrician said it passed."

Task 8 (level 3) You are the lead hand. You lead the drywall crew for Lakehead Interiors (fictional). It is day 9 in the swimlane plan. You arrive and see the insulation inspection sticker is not posted, and the vapour barrier is torn in two places.

a) Do you start boarding? Why or why not? b) Who do you tell, and what do you say? c) What can your crew do today instead?

Task 9 (level 3) Sequence a job. Put these steps for a new kitchen wall in order, from 1 to 7. Then mark the hold point with an H.

_ Electrical rough-in Drywall _ Framing Rough-in inspection _ Cabinets and finish _ Insulation and vapour barrier ____ Plumbing rough-in

Task 10 (level 3) Hallway argument. A drywaller from another company says to you, "Your boxes aren't done. We're boarding this wall now, you'll have to cut them out after." You are an electrical apprentice.

a) Write what you say to him, assertively and calmly. b) Who do you tell next? c) Why is boarding over the boxes a problem beyond your extra work?

Check yourself

  1. What is a dependency between trades?
  2. What is a hold point?
  3. Name two habits that keep the next trade moving.
  4. Who should sort out a problem between two companies' crews?
  5. One trade is one day late, and every trade after it waits on it. What happens to the finish date?
