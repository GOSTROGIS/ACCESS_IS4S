Respecting differences on a job site

Understand what culture is, work respectfully with people whose ways differ from yours, and know what BC human rights law says about work.

  • Learner
  • Instructor
  • Answers
  • Level: 1-2
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Value diversity and inclusivity of others; Work well with other people

Task 1 (level 1)

Open task. A good response: the learner teaches something real in about 3 minutes, checks that the partner can do or say it back, and reports one thing learned from the partner in a sentence. The learner chooses what to share. A trade trick counts as much as a family tradition.

Task 2 (level 1)

Open task. Sample lists:

  • Alike: we need food and rest, want to be treated fairly, want to go home safe, care about family, want to be good at our work.
  • Different: language, accent, faith, food, age, home community, how we were raised, how we show respect, how much we talk.

Two differences that matter on a crew (examples): language (safety instructions must be understood by everyone), faith (prayer times or holy days may affect the schedule), age (different experience with tools or technology), how people show respect (some people avoid direct eye contact with a boss, which is not rudeness).

Task 3 (level 2)

Open task. A good response names three real unwritten rules, sorts each one, and gives a practical change for one that leaves people out.

Examples

Unwritten rule Helps everyone or leaves out? Change
Whoever finishes first helps the next person Helps everyone Keep it
New people get the worst jobs until they "prove themselves" Leaves out Rotate the jobs, and pair new people with someone who shows them
Lunch talk is all jokes about one group Leaves out Lead says "not on this crew" and changes the subject
Ask the lead, not the other apprentice Can leave out Make it fine to ask anyone, and say so at the start of the job

Task 4 (level 2)

Open task. A good response is short, calm, does not attack the other person, and supports the person being targeted. Examples:

a) To the group, after the talk: "He covered everything we needed. Let's get to it." Later, to the co-worker who read: "Thanks for doing the talk." If it keeps happening, tell the lead.

b) "I can only speak for myself. People in my community have different views, and other Nations have their own. Here's what I think..." or "I'd rather not speak for anyone else."

c) "Smells good to me. What is it?" or simply "Come on, let them eat." Keep it light and change the subject.

d) As the lead: "Thanks for asking ahead. Let's work out when you'll make up the time." The lead does not ask her to explain her faith. The duty to accommodate means the employer looks at reasonable options.

Task 5 (level 2)

Case Ground involved? Ground
a) Sam Yes Age
b) Jo No A driver's licence is a real requirement for a driving job, not a protected ground
c) Ray Yes Indigenous identity
d) Lin Yes Religion. The employer also did not look at accommodation
e) Alex No Taste in music is not a ground in section 13. It may still be unfair and worth raising at work
f) Pat Yes A criminal conviction unrelated to the job

Steps: read the reason for the treatment in each case, then look for it in the section 13 list on the learner page.

Check yourself 1

Any answer along these lines: the shared beliefs, customs and ways of doing things of a group, such as language, food, ceremonies, values and how decisions are made.

Check yourself 2

First Nations, Inuit and Métis.

Check yourself 3

They are distinct peoples, and in BC alone there are 204 First Nations communities with 36 First Nations languages. One person can speak only for themselves, and putting them on the spot treats a whole people as all the same.

Check yourself 4

Any four of: Indigenous identity, race, colour, ancestry, place of origin, political belief, religion, marital status, family status, physical or mental disability, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, age (19 or older), a criminal or summary conviction unrelated to the job.

Check yourself 5

The employer's duty to take all reasonable steps to avoid a negative effect on a worker because of a protected ground, for example adjusting a schedule for a religious holy day.
