Respecting differences on a job site

Understand what culture is, work respectfully with people whose ways differ from yours, and know what BC human rights law says about work.

  • Learner
  • Instructor
  • Answers
  • Level: 1-2
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Value diversity and inclusivity of others; Work well with other people

Why this matters on the job

A BC crew can include people from different Nations, countries, faiths and generations, all on the same scaffold. You will eat lunch with them, depend on them for safety and learn the trade from them. If people feel judged or left out, they stop speaking up, and that is when mistakes get missed. Respecting differences is part of doing the job well.

Key ideas

Culture is the shared beliefs, customs and ways of doing things of a group of people. It shows up in language, food, clothing, music, art, how people mark a birth, a marriage or a death, what they value and how they make decisions. There is no single, fixed definition, because the things that make groups different are not the same everywhere.

Everyone has culture, and most people belong to more than one group at once: a family, a community or Nation, maybe a faith, a trade, a sports team, a crew.

Job sites have culture too. Every crew has unwritten rules: who sits where at lunch, how people joke, whether you can ask a "dumb" question, who gets the good jobs. Some of these rules help everyone. Some leave people out.

Indigenous peoples are not one group. The Canadian Constitution recognizes three Indigenous peoples: First Nations, Inuit and Métis. The Government of Canada describes them as distinct peoples with their own histories, languages, cultural practices and spiritual beliefs. In BC alone there are 204 First Nations communities and 34 distinct First Nations languages, according to the First Peoples' Cultural Council. So there is no single "Indigenous way," and no one person can speak for all Indigenous people.

Respect at work, in practice. The Skills for Success model asks you to:

  • understand that people from different cultures, backgrounds and abilities can have different customs, values and ways of thinking and acting
  • accept differences in characteristics, abilities, cultures, religions and values
  • respond without judging people for their different opinions, ideas and views
  • adapt to different styles of interaction when possible and appropriate

In plain words: ask instead of assuming, let people choose what they share about themselves, and don't make anyone the spokesperson for their group.

The law. BC's Human Rights Code (section 13) says a person must not refuse to employ someone, or discriminate against them about any part of their job, because of their:

  • Indigenous identity
  • race, colour, ancestry or place of origin
  • political belief
  • religion
  • marital status or family status
  • physical or mental disability
  • sex, sexual orientation, or gender identity or expression
  • age (19 or older)
  • a criminal or summary conviction that is unrelated to the job

Employers also have a duty to accommodate. The BC Human Rights Tribunal explains it as a duty to take all reasonable steps to avoid a negative effect on someone because of one of these grounds. For example, a rule to work a certain day can affect a person whose religion does not allow work on that day. The Tribunal advises workers to tell their employer if they can't meet a work requirement because of one of these personal characteristics.

Worked example: a call from home

North Arm Electrical (a fictional company) is on a tight schedule. Apprentice Shay gets a call: a family member has died, and the family is gathering in Shay's home community for the funeral. Shay expects to be away for several days.

Response A: "Can't you just come back after the funeral? It's one afternoon, right?" This assumes Shay's family does things the way the speaker's family does. It puts the schedule ahead of the person, and it asks Shay to defend their family's ways.

Response B: "I'm sorry. What do you need? Tell me roughly how long you'll be away, and I'll sort out coverage with the office." This does not ask Shay to explain the ceremony. It asks only what the job needs to know (how long), and it leaves the rest to Shay. The lead then checks the company's leave policy.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Tiny talents. In pairs, teach your partner something new in 3 minutes, then switch. It can be a word or phrase in a language you know, a game, a step in a recipe, a knot, a song line or a trick from your trade. You choose what to share. Then tell the group one thing you learned from your partner.

What I learned: __________

Task 2 (level 1) In a small group, make two lists: - ways people are alike (for example: we all need rest, we all want to be treated fairly) - ways people are different (for example: language, faith, food, age)

Circle two differences that could matter on a crew. Say why.

Task 3 (level 2) Write three unwritten rules from a crew, team or workplace you know. For each, decide: does it help everyone, or could it leave someone out? For one rule that leaves people out, suggest a change.

Task 4 (level 2) What would you say? Write a short, respectful response for each situation. You might be the person involved or someone standing nearby.

a) A co-worker reads the morning safety talk aloud. A few people laugh at his accent. b) A co-worker asks you: "You're Native, right? So what do Indigenous people think about pipelines?" c) At lunch, someone says loudly about a co-worker's food: "What is that? It stinks." d) A new worker asks the lead if she can leave 30 minutes early on Fridays for prayers, and make up the time on another day.

Task 5 (level 2) For each case, decide whether a ground in section 13 of the BC Human Rights Code is involved. If yes, name the ground.

a) Sam, 58, is told "we want someone younger" and is not hired. b) Jo is not hired as a delivery driver because Jo has no driver's licence. c) Ray is refused a labourer job because he is First Nations. d) Lin is let go because she can't work on her religion's holy day. The employer did not look at any other options. e) Alex is not offered overtime because the foreman doesn't like Alex's taste in music. f) Pat is turned down for a framing job because of a conviction 15 years ago that has nothing to do with the work.

Check yourself

  1. In your own words, what is culture?
  2. Name the three Indigenous peoples recognized in the Canadian Constitution.
  3. Why should you not ask one person to speak for all Indigenous people?
  4. Name four grounds protected in employment under the BC Human Rights Code.
  5. What is the duty to accommodate?
