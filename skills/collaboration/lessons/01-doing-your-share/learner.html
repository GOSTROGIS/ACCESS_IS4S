Doing your share on a crew

Know your role, know who depends on you, and build trust by doing what you said you would do.

  • Components: Work well with other people; Achieve a common goal with others

Why this matters on the job

On a BC job site, most work waits on someone else's work. A framer can't stand a wall until the plates are laid out. A drywall crew can't start until the electrical rough-in is done. When one person is late, or quietly skips a step, the whole crew loses time. A crew that trusts each other gets more done and works more safely.

Key ideas

Collaboration is "your ability to contribute and support others to achieve a common goal." That is the Government of Canada's wording in its Skills for Success model. Until 2021 this skill was called Working with Others. The new name covers more: it now includes inclusivity and respect for diversity.

Every crew member has a role. A role is the part of the job that is yours. To do your share, you need to know three things:

  1. What is my job today?
  2. Who is waiting on my work?
  3. Whose work am I waiting on?

Trust is built by small, everyday actions. The Skills for Success model lists "engage in trust building behaviours" and "adhere to social and organizational rules", for example, be on time. On a crew that looks like this:

  • Show up on time, with your gear.
  • Do what you said you would do.
  • If you can't, say so early, before it holds someone up.
  • Ask when you are not sure, instead of guessing.
  • Leave your work ready for the next person.

Know your strengths and where you need help. Nobody is good at everything. A strong crew knows who is fast at layout, who is careful with finishing, and who is still learning. Saying "I haven't done this before, can you show me once?" is part of doing your share.

Example: one pole, many hands

Is a totem pole made by one person? Here is one Haida pole project, as Parks Canada describes it.

The Gwaii Haanas Legacy Pole is a 42-foot monumental pole. It was commissioned by the Archipelago Management Board, where the Haida Nation and the Government of Canada have an equal number of members, to mark 20 years of managing Gwaii Haanas together.

  • A six-person committee (a hereditary Chief, two Haida citizens, a carver and two Gwaii Haanas staff) chose the design. Proposals were judged with no names attached.
  • The head carver was Jaalen Edenshaw, of the Ts'aahl Eagle clan. He chose the red cedar.
  • Faller Shaun Peacock worked with him to bring the tree down. The tree was trucked to Skidegate.
  • Jaalen asked Tyler York and his brother Gwaai Edenshaw to be his assistants. Tyler shaped the log with an Alaska mill. The carving was done in the carving shed at the Haida Heritage Centre.
  • On August 15, 2013, more than 400 people took part in raising the pole at Hlk'yah G̱awG̱a (Windy Bay) on Lyell Island. Elder Diane Brown blessed the pole. Four lines of people pulled it up, and final directions were given to make sure it stood straight.

So no: this pole took a design committee, a carver, a faller, assistants, an Elder's blessing and hundreds of people pulling on lines. Many parts had to be done before the next could start.

This describes one pole, made with the Haida Nation, as told on the Parks Canada page. It is not a rule for how every Nation or every pole works.

Worked example: a late start

Cedar Flats Framing (a fictional company) has a crew of four building a garage wall.

Person Role today Needs before they can start
Dana Journeyperson and crew lead, lays out the plates Nothing, starts at 7:00
Marcus Apprentice, cuts studs from the cut list The cut list, which is in his truck
Priya Apprentice, nails up the wall Laid-out plates from Dana and cut studs from Marcus
Tomas Labourer, stages material and keeps the area clear Nothing, starts at 7:00

Marcus is stuck in traffic and arrives at 7:20 without texting. For 20 minutes Priya has nothing to nail, and Dana stops her layout to look for the cut list.

A better way: at 6:45 Marcus texts Dana: "Stuck on Highway 1, there by 7:20. Cut list is on the dash of my truck, but I have a photo of it. Sending now." Dana can give the photo to Tomas, who starts cutting. Marcus is still late, but the crew keeps working. He told the lead early, said when he would arrive, and gave the crew what it needed.

The chart shows the same crew from Priya's point of view. Arrows point from the work you wait on to the work that waits on you.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Use the Cedar Flats table. For Dana, Marcus and Priya, write who is waiting on that person's work.

Task 2 (level 1) Sort these actions into two lists: builds trust and breaks trust.

a) Texting the lead before start time when you will be late b) Saying "yep, done" when you have not checked the job c) Putting tools back where the crew keeps them d) Taking the last box of screws without telling anyone e) Asking "Can you show me once?" before trying a task you have never done f) Leaving offcuts on the walkway for someone else to pick up g) Finishing your part and asking the lead what is next h) Arriving on time but without your safety boots

Task 3 (level 2) You are Marcus. It is 11:15 and the cut list is due at 12:00. You need 30 minutes beyond that deadline, plus your planned lunch break from 12:00 to 12:30. Your revised finish time is 13:00. You do not know which other materials are ready. Write a text to Dana. Say what is late, when it will be done, and ask Dana to confirm suitable work for the crew in the meantime.

Task 4 (level 2) Name one strength you bring to a crew. Name one task where you would want help the first time. Write the words you would use to ask for that help. You choose what to share.

Task 5 (level 2) Use the Legacy Pole facts. Name four different roles in the project. For each, name a step that had to be done before that person could do their part.

Task 6 (level 2) Use the chart of the Cedar Flats crew.

a) Whose work is Priya waiting on? b) Who is waiting on Priya? c) Marcus is 20 minutes late and does not text. Name everyone who loses time. d) Draw the same kind of chart for a job you know. Put yourself in the middle box.

Task 7 (level 3) You are the lead hand. Stoney Creek Concrete (a fictional company) has a pump truck booked for 7:30 to pour two footings. All rebar must be tied before the pump arrives. Your crew today:

Person Strength
Ray, journeyperson Fast at layout and tying rebar
Jess, second-year apprentice Ties rebar, was going to do the north footing
Ali, labourer, first week Moves material, has never tied rebar

At 6:50 Jess texts: "Flat tire. There by 7:40. Sorry."

a) Which task is now at risk, and why does it matter? b) Who takes over the north footing until Jess arrives? Give your reason. c) What job do you give Ali? d) Write the text you send back to Jess.

Check yourself

  1. What is the Skills for Success definition of collaboration?
  2. What was this skill called before 2021?
  3. Name three trust-building actions on a crew.
  4. You find out at 9:00 that you can't finish a task due at 10:00. When should you tell your lead, and what should you tell them?
  5. Does the Legacy Pole example tell you how every Nation makes a pole? Why or why not?
