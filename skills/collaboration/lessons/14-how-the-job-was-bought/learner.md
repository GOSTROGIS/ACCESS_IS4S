# How the job was bought and who answers to whom

All people, companies and jobs in this lesson are fictional. The facts about contracts, bonds and shop drawings come from the sources your instructor lists.

## Why this matters on the job

Lesson 9 showed the people on a site. But the lines between them depend on how the owner bought the job. On one job your design question goes through the general contractor to the architect. On another it stays inside one company. On another, your company's contract is with the owner, but a construction manager runs the site. If you know how the job was bought, you know where a question goes, who pays your company, and why a steel railing cannot be built until its shop drawing comes back stamped.

## Key ideas

### Three common ways to buy a building

| Way to buy | How it works | Who your company usually signs with | Where a design question goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Design-bid-build** | The owner hires designers (architect, engineers) first. They finish the drawings and specifications. Contractors bid. The owner signs with one general contractor (GC) to build it. | The GC (you are a subcontractor) | You, your foreman, the GC, then the consultant (usually by RFI, Communication lesson 12) |
| **Design-build** | The owner signs **one** contract with a design-builder, who both designs and builds. | The design-builder | You, your foreman, the design-builder, whose own designers answer |
| **Construction management** | The owner hires a construction manager (CM) early to give advice on cost, schedule and how to build it while the design is being done. The work is split into packages and each trade bids its own package. | Either the owner (when the CM gives services only) or the CM (when the CM also does the construction) | You, your foreman, the CM, then the consultant |

The standard Canadian forms of contract come from the Canadian Construction Documents Committee (CCDC). CCDC 2 is the stipulated price (fixed price) contract used on many design-bid-build jobs. CCDC 14 is a design-build stipulated price contract. CCDC 5A and CCDC 5B are construction management contracts.

**Safety does not change.** In BC, whatever way the job was bought, the prime contractor rule from lesson 9 still applies. If the owner has not named a prime contractor in a written agreement, the owner is the prime contractor.

### Bonds: promises backed by a surety

On many jobs, especially public ones, the owner asks contractors for **bonds**. A bond is a promise from a surety company that backs up the contractor.

| Bond | When | What it protects |
|---|---|---|
| **Bid bond** | With the bid | That the bidder is serious, will sign the contract at the price it bid, and will provide the performance and payment bonds. |
| **Performance bond** | After the contract is signed | The owner, if the contractor fails to do the work as the contract says. |
| **Labour and material payment bond** | After the contract is signed | Subcontractors and suppliers who have a **direct** contract with the bonded contractor, if they are not paid. Lower-tier subs and suppliers are not covered by the standard bond. |

The Surety Association of Canada says performance bonds and labour and material payment bonds are each typically 50% of the contract amount. The bond amount is the most the surety will pay.

### The shop drawing chain

Contract drawings show what the designer wants. A **shop drawing** is drawn by the fabricator or supplier to show exactly how it will make and install its part: sizes, materials, connections, field dimensions. A steel stair railing, a set of cabinets, a sprinkler layout and a rooftop unit all need shop drawings.

A shop drawing travels **up** the chain for review, then **back down**:

1. **Fabricator** draws it from the contract drawings and specifications.
2. **Subcontractor** checks it (if the fabricator works for a sub).
3. **General contractor** checks it: field measurements, quantities, fit with other trades. Then the GC stamps it and sends it on.
4. **Consultant** (architect or engineer) reviews it for **general conformance with the design**, then stamps it.
5. It goes **back down** the same way to the fabricator.

The consultant's stamp usually says one of these:

| Stamp | What the shop does next |
|---|---|
| Reviewed | Build it as drawn. |
| Reviewed as noted | Build it with the consultant's notes added. |
| Revise and resubmit | **Do not build.** Fix the drawing and send it up the chain again. |
| Not reviewed | The consultant did not review it. Ask your foreman before you build anything. |

Two things to remember:

- A shop drawing does **not** replace the contract documents. It adds detail to them.
- The consultant's review does **not** take away the contractor's responsibility for errors in the shop drawing. The shop still owns its measurements.

## Worked example: a handrail on a clinic fit-out

The Cedar Lane Health Society (owner) is fitting out a small clinic. It bought the job **design-bid-build**. Kestrel Architecture designed it. Tallgrass Builders won the bid and is the GC and prime contractor. Ironwood Fabrication has a subcontract with Tallgrass to supply and install the steel stair handrail.

**Bonds.** Tallgrass's contract is $640,000. On this job the owner asked for a performance bond and a labour and material payment bond, each for 50% of the contract.

50% of $640,000 = 0.5 × $640,000 = **$320,000** for each bond.

Ironwood has a direct subcontract with Tallgrass, so it is a claimant the payment bond covers if Tallgrass does not pay it.

**Shop drawing timeline.** On this job the specification gives the consultant 5 working days to review each shop drawing. The other times are Ironwood's and Tallgrass's own estimates, in working days:

| Step | Working days |
|---|---|
| Ironwood draws and checks the shop drawing | 4 |
| Tallgrass reviews and stamps it | 2 |
| Kestrel reviews and stamps it | 5 |
| Back down to Ironwood | 1 |
| **First pass total** | 4 + 2 + 5 + 1 = **12** |

Ironwood needs 8 working days to fabricate after the drawing comes back "Reviewed". Tallgrass needs the handrail on site by working day 25, counting from the day Ironwood starts drawing.

12 + 8 = **20 working days**. That is 5 days ahead of day 25.

**Where a question goes.** Ironwood's fitter sees that the stair stringer on site is 40 mm off the line on the drawing. He tells his foreman. Ironwood's foreman tells Tallgrass. Tallgrass sends an RFI to Kestrel. Nobody from Ironwood calls the architect directly, because Ironwood's contract is with Tallgrass.
