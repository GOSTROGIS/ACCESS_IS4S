Say no and ask for something in writing

Turn down a request in writing without losing the relationship, and write a formal request letter that answers the reader's worries and makes it easy for them to say yes.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Identify the task that requires you to write; Plan the writing task; Choose the appropriate language and style for the writing task; Choose the appropriate format for the writing task; Review and revise your writing

Session length: 90 min. All times below are part of the 90 minutes and match the steps in the learning tasks table.

Before class

This lesson builds on Communication lesson 4, Saying no and setting limits at work, and on Text and email a foreman or client. Refusing unsafe work is covered in Communication lesson 4 and follows a set legal process. It is not a "written no" in the sense of this lesson. If a learner asks, send them back to that lesson.

Print the request letter worked example so learners can label its parts. If you can, bring one real business letter in block style with the names and addresses covered.

Saying no to a boss, an older person or a client can be hard for some learners, and norms differ between families, communities and workplaces. Treat the five-part plan as one tool, not the only right way.

Opening (5 min, step 1.1)

Welcome learners. Read the weak reply to Ms. Grant aloud: "Hi Carol, hmm, maybe, the guys are pretty busy but we'll see what we can do, I'll let you know." Ask: "If you were Ms. Grant, are you getting a shed?" Most will say "maybe" or "yes". Ask: "What happens in two weeks when she asks when the shed is starting?" Say: "Today you learn to say no in writing so it is clear and kind, and to ask for something so the answer is more likely to be yes."

Objective (2 min, step 1.2)

By the end of the session, learners will be able to:

  • decide whether a message needs the written-no plan or the request plan (Identify the task that requires you to write)
  • plan a request by thinking through the reader's job, concerns, likely objection and benefit (Plan the writing task)
  • keep a firm, polite tone with a client, a supplier and a manager (Choose the appropriate language and style for the writing task)
  • use an email for a written no and a block-style letter for a formal request (Choose the appropriate format for the writing task)
  • find and fix a buried or over-explained no (Review and revise your writing)

Pre-assessment (3 min, step 1.3)

  1. "What is the hardest kind of request for you to say no to at work?"
  2. "If you wanted your boss to pay for a course, what would you put in your request?"

Post-assessment (5 min, step 4.1)

  1. Write the "no" sentence for a reply to a co-worker who asked to borrow the company truck for a weekend move.
  2. Name two of the four questions you answer about a reader before writing a request.
  3. In a block-style letter, what goes on the line that starts with "Re:"?

Summary (3 min, step 4.2)

During this session we covered:

  • the written no: thanks, reason, a clear no, an alternative, a friendly close
  • avoiding a buried no, over-explaining and blame
  • planning a request around the reader: their job, their concerns, their likely objection, the benefit to them
  • the request plan: the request first, background, details and benefit, the objection answered, a clear close
  • the parts of a block-style business letter
