# Say no and ask for something in writing

## Why this matters on the job

Some answers are better in writing. A client asks for extra work for free. A supplier wants to send a different size than the drawings call for. You want your employer to pay for a course or give you time to learn estimating. Writing gives you time to get the tone right, and it leaves a record of what was said. In Communication lesson 4, [Saying no and setting limits at work](../../../communication/lessons/04-saying-no-and-setting-limits/learner.html), you practised saying no out loud. This lesson puts a no, and a request, on paper.

## Key ideas

### 1. Two plans for two jobs

| You need to | Use the plan for |
|---|---|
| Turn down a request, give bad news | A written no |
| Get someone to agree to something | A written request |

### 2. The written no: five parts

1. **Thank or acknowledge.** Show you read the request.
2. **Give a short, true reason.** One or two sentences. No long list of excuses.
3. **Say no clearly.** The reader must not finish the message thinking you said "maybe".
4. **Offer an alternative,** if there is one you can live with.
5. **Close on a friendly, forward-looking note.**

What to avoid:

- Burying the no: "We'll see what we can do at some point." The reader hears yes.
- Over-explaining. Every extra excuse is something to argue with.
- Blame or sarcasm. The message may be forwarded.

When a client asks for extra work for free, the alternative is often to price it. See [Write a change request before you build it](../11-write-a-change-request/learner.html).

### 3. The written request: think about the reader first

Before you write, answer four questions:

| Question | Example |
|---|---|
| Who is the reader, and what is their job? | Dale Okafor, Shop Manager |
| What do they care about? | Cost, keeping the shop running, fewer mistakes |
| What will they likely object to? | "It costs money and you'll miss work on Fridays." |
| What is in it for them? | Fewer remade parts, an apprentice who can check drawings |

Then write five short paragraphs:

1. **The request,** in the first sentence or two.
2. **Background:** why you are asking now.
3. **The details and the benefit to the reader,** with numbers.
4. **The objection, answered** before they raise it.
5. **The close:** the exact decision you need, by when, and an offer to talk.

### 4. When to send a letter, and how it looks

Use a formal letter for requests that go in a file: training, time off, money, or a request to an organization you do not deal with every day. You can print and sign it, or attach it as a PDF to a short email.

A business letter in block style has every line starting at the left margin, a blank line between paragraphs, and these parts in order:

1. Your name and title (and your address if you write from outside the company)
2. The date
3. The reader's name, title and company (and address if it goes outside the company)
4. A subject line starting with "Re:"
5. A greeting: "Dear Mr. Okafor,"
6. The body
7. A closing: "Sincerely,"
8. Your signature, then your typed name and title

### Worked example: a written no

**Alder Creek Decks** (a fictional company) is finishing a deck for Carol Grant. She emails: "While your crew is here, could you also build a small garden shed? I assume it won't cost much since you already have the tools here."

A weak reply:

> Hi Carol, hmm, maybe, the guys are pretty busy but we'll see what we can do, I'll let you know.

The reply the owner sent:

> **Subject:** Your garden shed question
>
> Hello Ms. Grant,
>
> Thank you for thinking of us for your garden shed. We're glad you are happy with how the deck is coming along.
>
> Our crew starts another job on Tuesday, November 24, the day after your deck is finished, so we can't add the shed to this job.
>
> We would be glad to price the shed as a separate job for the spring. If you send me the size you have in mind and a photo of the spot, I will send you a price within a week.
>
> Thank you again. We look forward to finishing your deck on Monday.
>
> Lena Paul
> Owner, Alder Creek Decks

Find the five parts: thanks, reason, the no ("we can't add the shed to this job"), the alternative, the friendly close.

### Worked example: a formal request letter

**Kai Larsen** is an apprentice at **Fraser Bend Fabrication** (fictional). A local college runs a blueprint reading course on six Fridays, 4:00 to 7:00 pm, from January 8 to February 12, 2027. It costs $395. The shop day ends at 3:30 pm, which is too late to get there. Kai's letter:

> Kai Larsen
> Apprentice, Bay 2, Fraser Bend Fabrication
>
> 2026-11-30
>
> Dale Okafor
> Shop Manager, Fraser Bend Fabrication
>
> Re: Request for course fee and Friday schedule change, January 8 to February 12, 2027
>
> Dear Mr. Okafor,
>
> I am asking the shop to pay the $395 fee for a blueprint reading course, and to let me leave at 2:30 pm instead of 3:30 pm on six Fridays so I can get to the class.
>
> The course runs on Fridays from 4:00 to 7:00 pm, from January 8 to February 12, 2027. In October I misread a dimension on a shop drawing, and two brackets had to be remade. I want to be able to check drawings on my own before I cut.
>
> Reading drawings well means fewer remade parts and less checking time for the lead hands. Over the six weeks I would miss 6 hours of shop time. I will make up all 6 hours by starting 30 minutes early on Tuesdays and Thursdays in those same weeks. Rena Thomas has said that works for Bay 2.
>
> Could you let me know by Friday, December 11, so I can register before the course fills? I am happy to talk about it any time this week.
>
> Sincerely,
>
> (signature)
>
> Kai Larsen
> Apprentice

The likely objection (cost and missed Fridays) is answered in the third paragraph, with numbers.
