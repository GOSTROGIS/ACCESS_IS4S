Giving a short toolbox talk

Plan and deliver a five-minute safety talk to a crew, with a clear opening, a few key points, something to show, a check and a closing action.

  • Learner
  • Instructor
  • Answers
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Speak with clarity; Speak with purpose; Adapt to your audience and contexts

Why this matters on the job

Most BC crews hold short safety talks, often called toolbox talks or crew talks. In smaller workplaces that do not need a formal safety program, the BC Occupational Health and Safety Regulation (section 3.2) requires the employer to hold regular monthly meetings with workers to discuss health and safety, and to keep a record of them. Sooner or later you will be asked to lead one. A good five-minute talk can be the reason someone goes home without an injury.

Key ideas

A simple shape for a five-minute talk

  1. Open. Name the topic and why it matters here, today. "We're digging the trench for the water line this afternoon, so today's talk is about what's buried under us."
  2. Two or three key points. No more. People remember three things better than ten.
  3. Show something. A tool, a photo, the drawing, the sign on the wall. Showing helps people who learn by seeing and people whose first language is different from yours.
  4. Check. Ask a question that needs more than "yes": "What's the first thing you do if the lead hand asks you to dig before the locates are marked?"
  5. Close with one action. "Before you pick up a shovel today, check the locate marks with me."

Preparing

  • Pick one topic. Tie it to the work happening today or this week.
  • WorkSafeBC publishes free toolbox meeting guides on many topics at worksafebc.com/toolbox-meeting-guides. Many include a "Record of meeting" form.
  • Add local details: names, locations, what has happened on this site. WorkSafeBC's guides encourage presenters to add details specific to their own workplace.
  • Write your points on one card. Do not read a script word for word.
  • Practise once out loud and time it.

Delivering

  • Stand where everyone can see and hear you, away from running equipment.
  • Speak to the person farthest away.
  • Pause after each key point. Silence gives people time to think.
  • Keep your hands free to show things.
  • Look around the whole group as you talk, in the way that is comfortable for you.
  • Stand steady. Shifting from foot to foot, hands in pockets or arms crossed can make you look unsure, even when you are not.

Know your audience

A crew of experienced journeypeople does not need the basics explained. A crew of first-week apprentices does. Some crew members may have a different first language. Use plain words, show as well as tell, and check with questions.

Keep a record

In a small workplace's monthly safety meeting, the employer must keep a record of the meeting and what was discussed. WorkSafeBC's guideline says this does not have to be formal minutes. A record of when the meeting was held, who attended and the general topics is enough. Many crews use the record form on the back of a toolbox meeting guide for every talk.

Learning from strong speakers

Martin Luther King Jr. delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech on August 28, 1963, at the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom, at the Lincoln Memorial in Washington, D.C. Watch a short excerpt and notice how he uses pauses, repetition and his voice. You may also think of a speaker you respect from your own Nation, family or community, and what makes people listen to them.

Worked example

Rae, the foreman at Clearwater Roofing (a fictional company), plans a talk using WorkSafeBC's toolbox meeting guide "The right to refuse unsafe work."

Open: "Yesterday someone asked me if they could say no to a job they thought was unsafe. Good question. Today's talk is about your right to refuse unsafe work."

Point 1: "If you have reasonable cause to believe a task would create an undue hazard to you or anyone else, you must not do it. Stop and tell me right away. Use the words 'I'm refusing this as unsafe.'"

Point 2: "If we can't agree, we look at it again with our worker rep, Kai."

Point 3: "If it's still not sorted, we both call WorkSafeBC. Nobody gets disciplined for following these steps."

Show: holds up the guide and points to the three steps.

Check: "Who is our worker rep?" and "What are the exact words you use?"

Close: "If something up on that roof doesn't feel right today, stop and come get me."

Rae has everyone sign the record of meeting on the back of the guide. The talk takes about four minutes.

Practice

Task 1 (level 2) Watch a strong speaker. Watch the excerpt your instructor plays. Write what the speaker does with:

His voice: __________

Pauses: __________

Repetition: __________

His body and face: __________

Task 2 (level 1) Stand three ways. Say this line three times, standing a different way each time: "Today's talk is about ladders." First, slouch with your hands in your pockets. Second, cross your arms and look at the floor. Third, stand steady with your hands free and look around the group. The group says which version made them want to listen, and why.

Check yourself

  1. What are the five parts of a short toolbox talk?
  2. Why should a five-minute talk have no more than three key points?
  3. Under section 3.2 of the BC OHS Regulation, what must the employer in a small workplace do about health and safety meetings?
  4. Give two delivery habits that help a crew hear and follow you.
