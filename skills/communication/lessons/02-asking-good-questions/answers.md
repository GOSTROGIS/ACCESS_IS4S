# Asking good questions on a new crew

## Task 1 (level 1) Open or closed?

1. C. The answer is yes or no.
2. O. Starts with "how" and asks for an explanation.
3. C. Yes or no.
4. O. Starts with "what" and asks for a plan.
5. O, but a short one. "Who" asks for a fact, usually one name. Accept C if the learner explains that the answer is one name. The point is that it does not get a yes or no.
6. C. Yes or no.

## Task 2 (level 1) Open it up

Sample answers. Any open question that keeps the meaning is correct.

1. "Where do you want these?"
2. "Which saw should I use for this cut?"
3. "What did the inspector say?" or "When is the inspector coming?"
4. "When is break?"

## Task 3 (level 1) Name circle

This is a speaking task. It is complete when everyone has been introduced. A good response: the learner says their own name clearly, introduces the next person by name, and asks again without embarrassment if they forget.

## Task 4 (level 2) Your first-day questions

Answers vary. Check that there are at least two open and two closed questions and that each is marked correctly. Examples:

- "Where is the first aid station?" (O)
- "Who do I report to?" (O)
- "What PPE do I need on this site?" (O)
- "Is there an orientation I need to finish before I start?" (C)
- "Do I sign in at the trailer?" (C)
- "When are breaks?" (O)

A strong list includes at least one safety question.

## Task 5 (level 2) Keep it going

This is a role play. A good new worker:

- introduces themselves and uses the crew member's name
- asks at least two open questions (for Card 1: "How do I sign out a tool?" "Where do the batteries charge?")
- asks a follow-up that builds on an answer ("You said batteries charge in the cage. Who has the key?")
- lets the crew member finish each answer
- confirms with a closed question ("So I write my name and the time, right?")
- stays on work topics

The observer's checklist should match what actually happened, not how confident the new worker looked.

## Task 6 (level 2) Take out the blame

Sample answers.

1. "Can you walk me through why the panel goes there?" or "What decided the panel location?"
2. "What's holding this up?" or "Is there anything I can do to help move this along?"

A good answer asks for information and does not suggest the other person made a mistake.

## Check yourself

1. A closed question gets a short answer, often yes or no. An open question gets an explanation and usually starts with what, where, when, who, why or how.
2. When you want to confirm something you already think you know, such as an instruction you just heard.
3. "Sorry, tell me your name one more time?" Then use it.
4. Wait for a pause in the cut, then ask. A hazard is different: speak up right away.
