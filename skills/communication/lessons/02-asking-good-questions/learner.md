# Asking good questions on a new crew

## Why this matters on the job

Your first week on a new site, you do not know where the tools live, who signs off on what, or how this crew likes things done. The fastest way to learn is to ask. Good questions keep you safe, save rework and show the crew you care about doing it right. Asking is not a weakness. Guessing is the risk.

## Key ideas

**Start with your name.** A short introduction opens the door: "Hi, I'm Jordan. I'm the new first-year apprentice with Sam's crew." Then ask the other person's name and use it once: "Good to meet you, Ken."

**Forgetting a name is normal.** Most people forget a name right after they hear it, often because they are nervous. Ask again: "Sorry, tell me your name one more time?" Repeat it, use it in the conversation, or write it down later.

**Closed questions get a short answer.** Yes, no, or one fact. Use them to confirm.

- "Is this the panel marked TEMP 3?"
- "Do you want them stacked flat?"

**Open questions get an explanation.** They usually start with what, where, when, who, why or how. Use them to learn.

- "How do you want these stacked?"
- "What do I need to know about this site before I start?"

**Learn with open questions, then confirm with a closed one.** "How do you want the hangers spaced?" ... "So every four feet, right?"

**Keep a conversation going.** A conversation is not a speech. Ask a follow-up question that builds on what the person just said. Let them finish before you ask the next one.

**Keep it about the work at first.** With someone you have just met, stay on general topics: the job, the site, the weather, the commute. Leave personal questions until you know the person and they have shared first.

**Watch how a "why" question sounds.** "Why did you do it that way?" can sound like you think they did it wrong. "Can you walk me through how you set that up?" asks the same thing without judgement.

**Pick your moment.** Do not interrupt someone in the middle of a cut or a lift. Wait for a pause. The exception is safety: if you see a hazard, speak up right away.

### Worked example

First morning at Fraser Bend Mechanical (a fictional company). You meet Ken, a journeyperson plumber.

> You: "Hi, I'm Jordan, the new apprentice. Are you Ken?" (closed, to confirm)
>
> Ken: "Yep."
>
> You: "Good to meet you, Ken. What are we working on today?" (open)
>
> Ken: "Roughing in the drains in the duplex on the east side."
>
> You: "How do you want me to help with that?" (open)
>
> Ken: "Carry pipe and fittings from the trailer, then I'll show you how we cut and prep."
>
> You: "Where in the trailer are the fittings?" (open)
>
> Ken: "Back shelf, blue bins."
>
> You: "So pipe first, then the blue bins on the back shelf, then you show me cutting. Is that the order?" (closed, to confirm)
>
> Ken: "That's it."

Four questions gave Jordan the task, the location and the order. One closed question at the end confirmed it all.

**Task 3** (level 1) **Name circle.** Stand or sit in a circle. Say "My name is ..." then turn to the person on your right and say "and this is ..." That person does the same. Keep going until the circle is complete. If you forget a name, ask.

**Task 5** (level 2) **Keep it going.** Work in groups of three: a crew member, a new worker and an observer. The crew member picks a card and answers questions about it in character. The new worker keeps the conversation going for two minutes using what, where, when, who, why and how. The observer ticks the checklist. Rotate so everyone plays each role.

> **Card 1.** You run the tool crib on a big commercial site. Tools are signed out with a name and a time. Batteries charge in the cage at the back.
>
> **Card 2.** You are the site safety coordinator. You are showing a new worker the first aid station, the muster point by the main gate and the sign-in board.
>
> **Card 3.** You are a journeyperson drywaller. Today you are boarding ceilings in a daycare. Lifts are booked through the lead hand.

Observer checklist:

- [ ] introduced themselves and used the other person's name
- [ ] asked at least two open questions
- [ ] asked a follow-up that built on an answer
- [ ] let the crew member finish before asking the next question
- [ ] confirmed something with a closed question
- [ ] stayed on work topics

## Check yourself

1. What is the difference between an open question and a closed question?
2. When is a closed question the better choice?
3. You forgot the lead hand's name ten minutes after meeting her. What do you say?
4. Your journeyperson is halfway through a cut. You have a question about where to stack material. What do you do? What if your question were about a hazard?
