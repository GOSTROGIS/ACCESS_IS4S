# Break a job into a work breakdown structure

## Why this matters on the job

Before anyone orders lumber or books a crew, someone has to answer one question: what exactly are we building? A job that is broken into clear pieces is easier to price, schedule, hand out and check. A piece that nobody wrote down, like the stairs or the cleanup, is the piece that gets forgotten and costs money at the end. On BC sites, lead hands, estimators and superintendents break jobs down this way every week. This lesson comes before the activity lists in [Numeracy lesson 19](../../../numeracy/lessons/19-network-diagrams-and-logic/learner.html) and the planning steps in [lesson 7](../07-plan-and-organize-the-work/learner.html).

## Key ideas

### A work breakdown structure is a family tree for the job

A **work breakdown structure** (WBS) starts with the whole job at the top. Under it are the big pieces. Under each big piece are smaller pieces. You keep splitting until each piece is small enough to hand to one person or one crew.

### Pieces, not actions

A WBS lists the **pieces you will hand over** (the deliverables). Write them as things, not actions:

| Piece in the WBS (a thing) | Activity in the schedule (an action) |
|---|---|
| Pier footings | Pour pier footings |
| Stair stringers | Cut stair stringers |
| Railings | Install railings |

The activities come later, when you plan the order and the days. Start with the pieces so you do not miss any.

### Number every piece

The whole job is **1**. The big pieces are **1.1, 1.2, 1.3** and so on. The pieces under 1.2 are **1.2.1, 1.2.2** and so on. The number tells you where a piece sits in the tree. Some companies write the top as 1.0. Use whatever your company uses, the same way every time.

### The 100 percent check

Ask three questions of every WBS:

1. **Is everything in the job somewhere in the list?** Include the pieces people forget: permits and inspections, cleanup, handover.
2. **Is anything in the list that is not part of this job?** If the owner did not pay for it, it does not belong.
3. **Is any piece in two places?** Each piece has one home. If the door is under "walls" and also under "openings", it gets counted, priced and built twice on paper.

### Stop when a piece is a work package

Stop splitting when one person or crew can do the piece, you can say how long it takes and what it costs, and someone can check that it is done. That lowest piece is a **work package**. Write a card for it:

- **Number and name**
- **Who** does it
- **How long** it takes
- **Cost** (labour and material)
- **Done when**: what anyone can see or measure to know it is finished

### Worked example: the backyard deck

Riverbend Decks (a fictional company) is building the backyard deck you will meet again in Numeracy lesson 19.

```
1     Backyard deck
1.1     Footings
1.1.1     Layout and post holes
1.1.2     Pier footings
1.2     Frame
1.2.1     Posts and beams
1.2.2     Joists
1.3     Decking
1.4     Stairs
1.4.1     Stair stringers
1.4.2     Finished stairs
1.5     Railings
1.6     Close-out
1.6.1     Final inspection
1.6.2     Cleanup and handover
```

**100 percent check.** Everything the owner is paying for is there, including the inspection and the cleanup. Nothing extra is there: the owner's new hot tub is not in this contract, so it is not in the list. Each piece has one home.

**Work package card, 1.1.2 Pier footings** (job data for this fictional deck):

- **Who:** Marcus and one labourer
- **How long:** 1 day to pour, then 2 days for the concrete to cure before posts go on
- **Cost:** labour 2 workers × 6 hours × $40 an hour = $480, plus concrete, tubes and anchors $360, total **$840**
- **Done when:** 6 piers poured at the layout marks, tops at the marked height, anchors set, and the inspector's footing approval recorded (on this job the city checks the holes before the pour)

Notice the BEDMAS in the cost: multiply first (2 × 6 × 40 = 480), then add (480 + 360 = 840).

### From work packages to activities

Each lowest piece becomes one or more activities, written as a verb and a thing: 1.1.2 Pier footings becomes "Pour pier footings". When every work package has its activities, you have the activity list for the schedule. **Task 2** (level 2) Riverbend Decks is also building a small garden shed (fictional). Put these pieces into a WBS with numbers. The whole job is **1 Garden shed**. There are four big pieces: base, walls, roof and close-out.

> Floor framing, roof sheathing and shingles, gravel pad, wall framing, roof trusses, door and window, floor sheathing, siding, final cleanup, concrete deck blocks

**Task 4** (level 3) Northshore Pipe and Steel (fictional) has a job to build and install 10 sections of steel handrail for a stair in a small commercial building. Read the job notes.

> Shop drawings must be approved by the general contractor before we cut. Steel comes from our supplier. Each section takes 3 hours to cut and fit, 2 hours to weld and 1 hour to grind. Each section is then primed in the shop (half an hour each). Install on site takes 1.5 hours a section. After install, the general contractor checks the rail and we touch up any scratched primer. On this job, labour is costed at $50 an hour.

- a. Write a WBS with the whole job as **1** and at least five big pieces.
- b. Write a work package card for the piece that covers the 10 fabricated sections (cut, fit, weld, grind). Work out the hours and the labour cost with BEDMAS.
- c. How many hours of shop priming and site install are there for all 10 sections?

**Task 5** (level 2) Look at the backyard deck WBS in the worked example. For each lowest piece (the ones with three-part numbers, plus 1.3 and 1.5), write one activity as a verb and a thing. Then compare your list with the deck table in Numeracy lesson 19. Which piece in the WBS has no activity in that table? Why does it still belong in the WBS?
