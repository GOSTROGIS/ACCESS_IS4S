Troubleshoot a fault step by step

Make the equipment safe, describe the fault exactly, check the likely causes from simplest to hardest, and change one thing at a time.

  • Learner

  • Components: Identify the issue to address; Gather information to help you address the issue; Analyze the issue; Address the issue

Why this matters on the job

When a tool or machine stops working, the pressure is on to get the crew going again. Guessing and swapping parts can waste money, and working on equipment that still has power or pressure in it can injure you. A step-by-step method gets the fault found faster and keeps you safe. Employers in every BC trade want apprentices who can troubleshoot calmly and report clearly.

Key ideas

The troubleshooting steps

  1. Stop and make it safe. Stop using the equipment. Tell your supervisor. In BC, anyone who sees what appears to be an unsafe condition must report it to a supervisor or the employer as soon as possible (WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation section 3.10).
  2. Describe the symptom exactly. What happens, when it started, what changed just before, and what still works.
  3. Read the manual. Most manuals have a troubleshooting table with symptoms, possible causes and who may do each check.
  4. Check from simplest and most likely to hardest. Start with checks that take seconds and need no tools.
  5. Control the energy before you put your hands on it. See the next section.
  6. Change one thing at a time, test, and write it down. Otherwise you will not know what fixed it.
  7. Know where your role stops. If the fix needs covers or guards off, electrical work, or a part the manual says only a service centre may change, the equipment is removed from service and tagged so nobody uses it by mistake (section 12.10), and it goes to the person authorized to repair it.

Energy: unplug, release, lock out

WorkSafeBC counts electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, chemical and thermal energy as energy sources (section 10.1). If equipment could start up or release energy unexpectedly and hurt someone, that energy must be isolated and controlled (section 10.2).

  • Cord or quick-connect equipment (a portable compressor, a corded drill, a tool on a quick-release air line): unplug or disconnect it and keep the plug or connection point under your immediate control the whole time you work on it (section 10.11). Also release any stored pressure, as the manual says.
  • Hard-wired or fixed machinery (a table saw wired to a wall disconnect, a shop dust collector): lock out the energy isolating device with your own personal lock, following the written lockout procedure, and verify that the energy is locked out before you start (sections 10.3, 10.4 and 10.6). You remove your own lock when your work is done (section 10.7).

Worked example

A shop vac at Coldwater Fabrication (fictional) stops picking up. The symptom: it runs and sounds normal but has almost no suction, starting this morning after it was used on metal filings. The manual's table lists: full tank, clogged filter, blocked hose. Simplest first: unplug it and keep the plug beside you, then check the tank (half full), then look through the hose (a ball of filings and rag is stuck at the bend). Clear the hose, plug in, test: suction is back. Record: "Shop vac, no suction. Cause: hose blocked at the bend. Cleared, tested OK, 9:15 a.m."

Practice

Task 1 (level 2) Put the troubleshooting steps in order: test and record the result, describe the symptom exactly, stop and tell the supervisor, unplug and release the pressure, read the manual's table, check the simplest likely cause first.

Task 2 (level 2) Use the crew messages to write the symptom description in two or three sentences, with facts only. Then name the message that is an opinion or a guess, and say why you would not act on it yet.

Task 3 (level 2) Using the manual's table and the crew messages, put the four possible causes for "runs all the time" in the order you would check them. Give a reason for your order.

Task 4 (level 2) For each action, write A (operator may do it with the compressor running), B (operator may do it only after page 4: off, unplugged, plug kept with you, tank at 0 psi) or C (not the operator's job: remove from service, tag it, and send it to the authorized service centre).

  • a. Read the regulator gauge.
  • b. See whether the tank drain valve is closed.
  • c. Replace the damaged coupler on hose 2.
  • d. Take off the pump cover to look at the valves.
  • e. Look at the intake filter.
  • f. Listen for leaks with the nailers connected but not firing.

Task 5 (level 3) Here is the crew's test log. Decide whether the fault is fixed, and what should happen next.

Time Change made Test result
3:00 p.m. None. Checked the drain valve. Drain valve was already closed. Compressor still runs nonstop.
3:05 p.m. Followed page 4. Replaced the coupler on hose 2. Plugged back in. Compressor reached 125 psi and stopped.
3:25 p.m. None. Nailers connected, not firing, for 20 minutes. Compressor did not restart. Tank gauge still above 95 psi.
3:30 p.m. Regulator left at 90 psi. Ten test nails into a scrap stud. All ten nails flush.
  • a. Is the fault fixed? Use the log to explain.
  • b. Why did the crew check the drain valve even though they could hear the hiss at hose 2?
  • c. Bri now says, "While we're at it, swap the intake filter and hose 2 too." What do you say, and why?
  • d. What did the log prove about Bri's 2:40 p.m. message?

Task 6 (level 2) Next week you need to change the blade on the shop's table saw, which is wired to a disconnect switch on the wall. Why does the "unplug and keep the plug with you" method not apply? List what you must do instead before you start, using the sections named in the key ideas.
