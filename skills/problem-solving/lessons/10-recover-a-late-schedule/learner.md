# Recover a late schedule

## Why this matters on the job

Every job hits a problem: a failed inspection, a late delivery, a sick crew, a week of rain. The schedule slips. A good lead hand or superintendent does not just say "we'll be late". They work out how late, find ways to buy the time back, weigh the cost and the risk of each, and pick the best one. This lesson uses the critical path skills from Numeracy lessons 17 to 21 and the decision steps from lesson 6 of this set.

## Key ideas

### Six steps to recover a late schedule

1. **Measure the delay.** Put the delay into the network and redo the passes. How many working days late is the finish? What date is that?
2. **Find the new critical path.** Only time saved on the critical path moves the finish date.
3. **List your options.** Look for all four kinds below. Write down how many days each saves, what it costs and what could go wrong.
4. **Check the musts.** Drop any option, or mix of options, that does not meet a must, for example the date the owner needs.
5. **Choose and justify.** From the options that meet the musts, pick the one with the best mix of cost, risk and effort. Plan a backup with if-then thinking.
6. **Check the result and tell people.** Redo the passes with your choice. Look at the float that is left. Tell the owner and every trade whose dates change.

### Four ways to buy time back

| Option | What you do | Why it saves time | Costs and risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Resequence** | Change the order of the work or split it, so crews keep working around the problem. | Work that was waiting starts sooner. | Low cost. Needs planning. Crews may set up twice. Some changes need the inspector or engineer to agree. |
| **Add crew** (crashing) | Put more workers or equipment on a critical activity. | The activity takes fewer days. | Extra labour cost. Only helps if there is room to work and the work can be shared. New workers need an orientation. |
| **Overtime** | Work longer days or a Saturday. | More working hours fit into the same calendar days. | Overtime pay or a premium from the subcontractor. Fatigue raises the risk of mistakes and injuries. |
| **Fast-track** | Overlap activities that were planned one after the other. | The second activity starts before the first one finishes. | Little direct cost. Higher risk of rework, damage and crowding. |

**Shorten the critical path only.** Buying time on an activity with float does not move the finish date. After each change, check the critical path again. A new path may become critical.

### Worked example: late roof trusses on the garage

Northshore Garage Builders (fictional) planned the garage in Numeracy lessons 18 to 21 at 20 working days, finishing on Monday, June 7, 2027.

1. **Measure.** The truss supplier says the trusses will take 14 working days instead of 10. The trusses had 3 days of total float, so the job is 14 − 10 − 3 = **1 day late**: 21 days, Tuesday, June 8.
2. **New critical path.** 1, 6 (trusses), 11, 12. The trusses are now critical. The footing, slab and framing work (activities 2 to 5 and 7 to 10) now has 1 or 2 days of float.
3. **Options.**
    - Resequence: nothing useful. The roof cannot go on before the trusses arrive.
    - Add crew: a second roofer cuts Shingles from 2 days to 1 for $350 (from lesson 21). Saves 1 day.
    - Overtime: the framing crew works a Saturday. But framing is no longer critical, so it saves nothing.
    - Fast-track: start shingling while sheathing is still going on. Risky on a small roof with two crews on it.
4. **Musts.** The owner needs June 7. Only the extra roofer meets it with low risk.
5. **Choose.** Add the second roofer for $350. If the supplier slips again, then ask about a Saturday for the roof crew.
6. **Check.** With trusses at 14 days and shingles at 1 day, the passes give **20 days**, Monday, June 7. Every activity from 11 on is critical, so the roof crew must not lose a day.

### Musts and wants

From lesson 6: a **must** is a requirement that an option has to meet, or it is out. A **want** is something you would like, used to compare the options that are left, for example lowest cost, lowest risk or no weekend work.

**The options.** The site supervisor and the trades suggest four ideas. Costs are made up for this lesson.

- **Option A.** The inspector agrees that the walls without plumbing passed. The crew insulates and boards those walls during the drain repair, and does the shower wall after the re-inspection. Tape and mud can start on the finished walls. Extra cost: the crews set up twice, about **$240**.
