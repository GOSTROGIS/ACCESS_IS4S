# Find the biggest problem first

## Why this matters on the job

At the end of a job, the deficiency list can run to dozens of items. In a shop, the reject bin fills with parts that failed for all sorts of reasons. A crew cannot fix everything at once. The trick is to find the one or two problems that cause most of the trouble and fix those first. Builders and fabricators across BC use four simple tools for this: a tally sheet, a Pareto chart, a fishbone diagram and Plan, Do, Check, Act. This lesson builds on the patterns in [lesson 4](../04-spot-patterns-and-causes/learner.html) and the before-and-after rates in [lesson 8](../08-check-whether-it-worked/learner.html).

## Key ideas

### 1. Tally the problems

Sort each problem into a type and make a mark for each one. Count the marks. A **tally sheet** turns a long, messy list into a few numbers.

### 2. Sort them in a Pareto chart

A **Pareto chart** is a bar chart with the tallest bar on the left and the shortest on the right. "Other" always goes last. A line above the bars shows the **running total percent**: the first bar, then the first two together, and so on.

Very often a few types make up most of the problems. The chart shows them at a glance, so the crew works on those first.

### 3. Draw a fishbone for the biggest one

A **fishbone diagram** (also called a cause-and-effect diagram) puts the problem at the head of the fish. Each bone is a family of possible causes:

- **People:** training, experience, rushing, communication
- **Method:** the order of the work, the procedure, the checklist
- **Material:** what was delivered, stored or used
- **Equipment:** tools, machines, carts, blades
- **Environment:** space, light, weather, noise

Write every possible cause on a bone. Then pick the one or two most likely and check them. A fishbone gives you ideas to test, not answers.

### 4. Test a fix with Plan, Do, Check, Act

- **Plan:** choose one change, say what you expect, and decide how you will measure it.
- **Do:** try it on a small scale, such as one area or one week.
- **Check:** compare the results with the plan. Use rates, not just counts (lesson 8).
- **Act:** if it worked, make it the new standard way. If not, learn from it and plan the next try.

Then go round again with the next biggest problem.

Some factories have a cord or a button any worker can use to stop the line and call a lead hand when something is wrong. The idea is the same: fix the problem where it starts instead of passing it down the line.

### Worked example: a deficiency list

Harbourline Interiors (a fictional company) finished a small office fit-out. The walk-through found 80 deficiencies. The site lead tallied them.

| Type | Count | Percent of total | Running total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Drywall dents and gouges | 32 | 40% | 40% |
| Paint touch-ups | 20 | 25% | 65% |
| Missing or loose trim | 12 | 15% | 80% |
| Doors that do not latch | 8 | 10% | 90% |
| Missing cover plates | 4 | 5% | 95% |
| Other | 4 | 5% | 100% |
| **Total** | **80** | **100%** | |

Percent of total = count ÷ 80 × 100. For drywall: 32 ÷ 80 × 100 = 40%.

Drywall and paint together are 65% of the list. The site lead draws a fishbone for **drywall dents**:

- **People:** new labourers carry material through finished halls.
- **Method:** drywall is finished before the doors and trim go in, so carts pass bare corners for weeks.
- **Material:** no corner guards were ordered.
- **Equipment:** the material carts have hard steel edges.
- **Environment:** the main hallway is narrow, and one end is badly lit.

The crew thinks carts hitting outside corners is the most likely cause. **Plan:** put plastic corner guards on every outside corner in hallway A only, and count new dents for two weeks in hallway A and in hallway B, which is the same length. **Do:** install the guards. **Check:** hallway A had 3 new dents, hallway B had 11. **Act:** add corner guards to every job's start-up list, and move on to the next problem, paint touch-ups.

## Practice

**Task 1** (level 1) Northshore Pipe and Steel (fictional) kept a log of 40 rejected parts over two weeks. The codes are: **B** = burrs not ground off, **L** = wrong length, **H** = hole in the wrong place, **U** = weld undercut, **P** = paint runs. Make a tally sheet and count each code.

| Parts | Reject codes |
|---|---|
| 1 to 10 | B, U, B, L, L, B, H, L, B, B |
| 11 to 20 | P, B, H, L, P, L, L, H, H, H |
| 21 to 30 | L, U, B, H, P, B, B, B, U, L |
| 31 to 40 | H, U, L, B, U, B, B, H, B, L |

**Task 2** (level 2) Use your counts from Task 1.

- a. Make a Pareto table with count, percent of total and running total.
- b. Sketch the Pareto chart.
- c. Which two types make up more than half of the rejects?

**Task 3** (level 2) The shop chose **burrs not ground off** for a fishbone. Here are notes from a crew huddle. Put each note on the right bone: people, method, material, equipment or environment.

- a. "The new apprentices were never shown how to check an edge for burrs."
- b. "We grind at the end of the day when everyone is rushing to pack up."
- c. "Flap discs run out by Wednesday, so people use worn ones."
- d. "The cold saw blade is dull, so every cut leaves a big burr."
- e. "The light over the grinding bench has been out for a month."
- f. "The cut list does not say to deburr."
- g. "The grinder at station 2 is missing its side handle."

One of these notes needs action today, before any fishbone work. Which one, and why?

**Task 4** (level 3) The shop runs one Plan, Do, Check, Act cycle on burrs.

- **Plan:** replace the dull cold saw blade, change nothing else, and count burr rejects for two weeks.
- **Do:** before the change, 14 of 200 parts made were rejected for burrs. In the two weeks after, 3 of 150 parts made were rejected for burrs.

- a. Work out the burr reject rate before and after, as percentages.
- b. Did the change help? Why can the shop say the blade made the difference?
- c. Write the **Act** step.

**Task 5** (level 3) Go back to Harbourline's 80 deficiencies. The site lead now looks at **cost to fix** instead of count. On this job the average cost to fix each one is: drywall $15, paint $10, trim $20, doors $90, cover plates $5, other $25.

- a. Work out the total cost for each type and the total for all 80.
- b. Which type is the biggest problem by cost? What percent of the total cost is it, to one decimal place?
- c. Should the crew fix drywall first or doors first? Explain in two sentences.
