# Decide whether an online answer can be trusted

## Why this matters on the job

When you get stuck on a job, the fastest answer is usually on your phone: a search, a video, a forum or an AI chatbot. Some of those answers are excellent. Others are out of date, written for another country, or trying to sell you something. In BC trades, acting on the wrong rule can fail an inspection, cost money or hurt someone. Checking takes a few minutes.

## Key ideas

### Five questions for any answer

1. **Who** wrote it? A regulator, a manufacturer, a trade school, a stranger, a company selling something?
2. **When** was it written? Codes and rules change. An answer from before the current edition may be wrong now.
3. **Where** does it apply? Rules in another province or country can be different from BC's.
4. **Why** was it written? To inform, to sell, to get views?
5. **Can I check it** against the official source?

### Warning signs

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security suggests looking for: a web address with typos or one that does not match the organization it claims to represent, no contact information, no physical address and no "About us" page, and information that is out of date. A .net or .org ending alone does not show that a site is unreliable. Compare the full domain with the organization's verified address. Check the story against other sources, and do not assume something is correct just because a friend sent it.

### AI chatbots

AI tools can give a confident, well-written answer that is wrong. The Cyber Centre advises being careful with AI content, analyzing it before you act on it, and validating the sources to check whether it is accurate. Use AI to get started or to find the name of the rule, then read the rule itself.

### Official BC sources for the trades

| Topic | Official source |
|---|---|
| Workplace health and safety | WorkSafeBC, Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) Regulation: https://www.worksafebc.com/en/law-policy/occupational-health-safety/searchable-ohs-regulation/ohs-regulation |
| Building and plumbing | Province of BC, BC Building Code 2024: https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/industry/construction-industry/building-codes-standards/bc-codes/2024-bc-codes |
| Electrical | Province of BC, BC Electrical Code: https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/industry/construction-industry/building-codes-standards/bc-codes/electrical-code |
| A specific tool or machine | The manufacturer's manual for that model |
| How your employer does it | Your supervisor and the employer's written procedures |

A few current facts from those pages:

- The **BC Building Code 2024** came into effect on March 8, 2024. Its earthquake design and adaptable dwelling requirements apply to projects with building permits applied for on or after March 10, 2025.
- The **BC Electrical Code** is the Canadian Electrical Code, Part I, adopted with a few BC changes. The current edition, the 26th edition (2024), came into effect in BC on March 4, 2025. Technical Safety BC enforces it.

Projects permitted before a new edition can fall under transition rules, so check the official page or ask your supervisor which edition applies to your job.

### Worked example

Your search for "do I need to lock out an air compressor" finds a forum post: "Nah, just turn it off, it's only air." Check it:

- **Who:** an unnamed forum user. **When:** no date. **Where:** unknown. **Why:** a quick reply.
- **Official source:** WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation Part 10 lists **pneumatic** energy as an energy source (section 10.1). Where unexpected startup or release of energy could injure someone, it must be isolated and controlled (section 10.2). A corded compressor can be worked on without a lock when it is unplugged and the plug is kept under your immediate control the whole time (section 10.11). The manual will also tell you to release the stored air pressure.
- **Result:** the forum post is wrong. Do not use it.

## Practice

**Task 1** (level 2) You want to know: "What eye protection do I need for grinding at work in BC?" Rank these sources from most to least trustworthy for this question, and give a reason for each.

- a. A forum post from 2014 on a United States woodworking site
- b. A video by a tool reviewer, paid by a safety glasses brand
- c. An AI chatbot answer that names no sources
- d. WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation section 8.14
- e. The manual for your grinder
- f. Your supervisor and your employer's written safety rules

**Task 2** (level 2) An AI chatbot gives you this answer:

> "In BC, safety glasses for work must meet ANSI Z87.1. CSA-certified glasses are not accepted."

Open WorkSafeBC OHS Regulation section 8.14 at https://www.worksafebc.com/en/law-policy/occupational-health-safety/searchable-ohs-regulation/ohs-regulation/part-08-personal-protective-clothing-and-equipment and check the answer. What is right, what is wrong, and what does the chatbot leave out?

**Task 3** (level 2) You find this web page. List every warning sign, and check the main claim against WorkSafeBC Part 10.

> **worksafe-bc-rules.net**
>
> **BC Safety Rules Made Simple!**
>
> Good news: lockout is only needed on machines over 600 volts. Everything else, just hit the off switch. Buy our complete lockout kit today, 50% off!
>
> (No author, no date, no contact page.)

**Task 4** (level 2) A blog post from 2022 says:

> "Remember, BC builds to the 2018 BC Building Code, and BC electrical work follows the 2021 Canadian Electrical Code."

Use the table above to check both claims. What would you say to an apprentice who quoted this post on a new job today?

**Task 5** (level 3) Make your own check card with the five questions. Then pick a real question from your trade, for example about fall protection, a ladder, a stair or a tool. Find the official BC source that answers it, write down its web address, and write the answer in your own words.
