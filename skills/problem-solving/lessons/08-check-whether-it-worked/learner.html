Problem solving · Lesson 08 · Learner

Check whether the fix worked

Decide what success looks like, test the fix under real conditions, follow up, ask for feedback and record what you learned.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 60 min
  • Components: Address the issue; Evaluate the effectiveness of the solution or decision

Why this matters on the job

A fix that works for ten minutes is not finished. On BC sites, a leak that comes back, a door that sticks again in winter or a repair that causes a new problem costs a return trip and trust. Checking the result, following up and sharing what you learned is how crews stop solving the same problem twice.

Key ideas

Decide what success looks like before you test

Write checks that anyone can observe. "It works" is not a check. "The compressor reaches 125 psi and shuts off" is.

Test under real conditions

Test the way the work is really done: with the real tools, the real load, for long enough. A door that latches once in the morning may not latch in the afternoon heat.

Watch for side effects

A fix can solve one problem and start another. Ask: Did anything else change?

Follow up

Come back later: the next day, the next week, or after the next cold snap. Problems that return are telling you the first cause was not the whole story.

Count carefully

When you compare before and after, compare rates, not just counts: 2 failures out of 24 is different from 2 out of 200. Small numbers can change a lot by chance, so keep checking before you claim success. If you changed two things at once, you cannot say which one worked.

Ask for feedback and record what you learned

The people who use the fix every day notice things you miss. Ask them. Then write a short lesson learned: what happened, what worked, and what to do next time. The ACCESS decision steps end the same way: follow up, evaluate, and revise if necessary.

Worked example

Back in the shop vac example from the troubleshooting lesson, the hose was cleared and suction came back.

  • Success checks: picks up a handful of filings from the floor in one pass, runs 10 minutes with no drop in suction, filter not torn.
  • Follow-up: next morning, the hose blocked again at the same bend.
  • New question: why does it block there? The crew had been vacuuming filings and rags together.
  • Revised fix: rags go in the bin by hand, and the shop vac is for filings only.
  • Lesson learned: "Shop vac hose blocks at the bend when rags are vacuumed. Pick up rags by hand."

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) In the troubleshooting lesson, Riverbend Framing (fictional) replaced the coupler on hose 2 of their compressor. Write four checks that would show the fix worked. Each must be something anyone could see or measure.

Task 2 (level 2) Here is the follow-up log for the compressor.

Day What happened
Wednesday 7:30 a.m. Compressor reached 125 psi and shut off. Nailers working normally.
Wednesday 2:00 p.m. Compressor running longer than usual. Hiss at the coupler on hose 3.
Thursday 7:00 a.m. Crew followed page 4 of the manual and replaced the coupler on hose 3. Compressor normal all day.
Friday 7:30 a.m. Chris points out that the couplers on hoses 1 and 4 are the same brand and age as the two that failed.
  • a. Did the Tuesday fix work? Did it solve the whole problem? Explain.
  • b. What pattern does the log show?
  • c. Write one short-term action and one long-term action for the crew.
  • d. Is there anything from Monday's event (the bin truck backing over hose 2) that should go in the long-term plan?

Task 3 (level 2) Write three questions you would ask the framing crew to get feedback on the compressor and hoses. Then name one other person whose feedback would help, and say why.

Task 4 (level 3) In the patterns lesson, Northshore Pipe and Steel (fictional) found that night-shift welds in booth 3 failed the bend test far more often: 9 out of 24 samples. The supervisor then made two changes to booth 3: a screen was moved to block the draft from the overhead door, and a damaged ground cable was replaced. Here are the next two weeks.

Shift Booth Samples tested Samples failed
Day 1 and 2 24 1
Night 3 24 2
  • a. What percentage of night-shift samples failed before the changes, and after?
  • b. By how many percentage points did the night-shift failure rate change?
  • c. Which of these statements does the record support? Explain each.
    • "The screen fixed the problem."
    • "The failure rate in booth 3 was lower in the two weeks after the changes."
    • "Booth 3 is now as good as booths 1 and 2."
  • d. What would you do next?

Task 5 (level 2) Write a lesson learned of four or five sentences for the next crew toolbox talk at Riverbend Framing, based on the compressor and hoses. Say what happened, what worked, and what to do next time.

Check yourself

  1. Why should you write success checks before you test?
  2. What is a side effect of a fix?
  3. Why compare rates instead of counts?
  4. What is the problem with making two changes at once?

More practice: close out the job and record lessons learned

A job is not finished when the last board goes down. It is finished when every item on the punch list (the list of small things still to fix or hand over) is closed, the owner has what they need, and the crew has written down what to keep, what to change and what to try next time. That short record is how the next crew starts ahead.

Task 6 (level 2) Riverbend Decks (fictional) is closing out a backyard deck, the same deck used in Numeracy lesson 19. Here is the punch list on Monday morning.

No. Punch list item Status
1 Touch up stain on the stair treads Done
2 Tighten the loose baluster on the east railing Done
3 Replace one cracked deck board Open: new board arrives Thursday
4 Pick up offcuts and loose screws from the lawn Done
5 Give the owner the stain and hardware product sheets Done
6 Final inspection passed Done
7 Fill and re-seed the ruts where the truck drove on the lawn Open
8 Return the rented auger Done

Notes the crew wrote during the job:

"Footing pour got rained out. Lost 2 days." "Ordering lumber a week early meant we never waited for material." "The cracked board came in the first delivery. Nobody checked the pile." "Owner was really happy to get the product sheets." "Truck wrecked the lawn. Should have laid down plywood."

  • a. How many items are done? What percent of the punch list is complete?
  • b. Which items stop the job from being closed out? For each one, name an owner (a job title is fine) and a date to have it done.
  • c. Write a lessons-learned record under three headings: Keep (worked well, do it again), Change (caused a problem, do it differently) and Try next time (a new idea). Put at least one item under each heading, using the crew's notes.
  • d. Who should get a copy of the record, and why?
