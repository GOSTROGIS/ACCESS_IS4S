# Check whether the fix worked

## Task 1 (level 1)

Any four observable checks, for example:

- The compressor reaches 125 psi and shuts off.
- With nailers connected but not firing, it stays off for at least 20 minutes.
- No hiss can be heard at the hose 2 coupler.
- At 90 psi, the nailers drive nails flush in a scrap stud.
- The next morning, the compressor still reaches 125 psi and shuts off.

"It works" or "the crew is happy" are not observable checks.

## Task 2 (level 2)

- a. Yes, the Tuesday fix worked for hose 2: on Wednesday morning the compressor was normal. It did not solve the whole problem, because a new leak appeared at the hose 3 coupler that afternoon.
- b. Couplers of the same brand and age are failing one after another.
- c. Short term, for example: inspect the couplers on hoses 1 and 4 today and replace any that leak, following page 4. Long term, for example: replace all couplers of that brand and age, add a coupler check to the weekly equipment inspection, or try a different coupler and track it.
- d. Yes. The bin truck crushed hose 2 on Monday. A long-term plan should keep hoses out of vehicle routes or protect them where they cross, and report the crushing to the supervisor so site traffic can be managed.

## Task 3 (level 2)

Any three open, useful questions, for example:

- "Have you heard any hissing or noticed the compressor running longer since Thursday?"
- "Are the nailers sinking nails the way they should?"
- "Where do the hoses get driven over or caught?"
- "Is there anything about the setup that slows you down?"

Another person, for example: the supervisor (decides on buying new couplers or hoses and on site traffic), the person who maintains the equipment (knows its service history), or the supplier (knows whether that coupler has known problems).

## Task 4 (level 3)

- a. Before: 9 ÷ 24 = 0.375 = **37.5%**. After: 2 ÷ 24 = 0.083, about **8.3%**.
- b. 37.5 − 8.3 = **29.2 percentage points** lower.
- c.
  - "The screen fixed the problem." **Not supported.** Two changes were made at once, so the record cannot say whether the screen, the cable, or both made the difference.
  - "The failure rate in booth 3 was lower in the two weeks after the changes." **Supported.** It went from 37.5% to about 8.3%.
  - "Booth 3 is now as good as booths 1 and 2." **Not supported.** Night shift in booth 3 was 2 of 24 (about 8.3%), and day shift was 1 of 24 (about 4.2%). With only 24 samples each, one or two failures either way can change the rate a lot. More weeks of records are needed.
- d. Keep testing and recording for several more weeks. Do not remove a protective screen or reinstall a damaged cable to recreate a fault. If further cause testing is needed, the supervisor should arrange a controlled, safe comparison that keeps required safeguards in place. Ask the night welders for feedback.

## Task 5 (level 2)

Points a good response covers:

- what happened: the compressor ran nonstop and the nailers were weak, because couplers were leaking, first on hose 2 after it was run over and then on hose 3
- what worked: describing the symptom exactly, checking the simplest causes first, following page 4 of the manual, changing one thing at a time
- what to do next time: check couplers each week, keep hoses out of vehicle paths, report damage right away, and do not assume the pump is the problem without checking
- plain, short sentences a crew would understand

## Check yourself

1. So you know ahead of time what you are looking for, and you cannot talk yourself into calling a weak result a success. (Level 2)
2. A new problem caused by the fix. (Level 1)
3. Because the number of tries can be different. 2 failures out of 24 is a much higher rate than 2 out of 200. (Level 2)
4. If things improve, you cannot tell which change made the difference. (Level 2)

## Task 6 (level 2)

a. **6** items are done (1, 2, 4, 5, 6 and 8). 6 ÷ 8 × 100 = **75%** complete.

b. Items **3** (cracked deck board) and **7** (ruts in the lawn). Example owners and dates:

- Item 3: lead hand, replace the board on Thursday when it arrives, or Friday at the latest.
- Item 7: lead hand or labourer, fill and re-seed the ruts this week, before the owner's final walk-through.

Any named owner and specific date is accepted. "Someone, soon" is not.

c. Example record:

- **Keep:** order lumber a week early. Give the owner the product sheets at handover.
- **Change:** check every delivery pile for damaged boards when it arrives, before the driver leaves. Plan the footing pour around the forecast and keep tarps on site.
- **Try next time:** lay plywood or ground mats along the truck route to protect the lawn.

A good record is short, uses plain words a new crew member would understand, and turns each note into an action, not a complaint.

d. The owner of the company or the supervisor (to update how jobs are run), the next crew or lead hand who builds a deck (so they start with the lessons), and whoever orders material and books deliveries (for the delivery check and the early order). Keeping a copy in the job file means the lesson is not lost when people move on.
