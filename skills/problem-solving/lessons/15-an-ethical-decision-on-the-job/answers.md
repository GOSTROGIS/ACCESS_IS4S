## Task 2 (level 2)

a. Example: "Signing for 10 hours when the crew worked 8 would bill the client for work that was never done, but saying no could cost me my place on the crew."

b. Extra hours: (10 − 8) × 4 workers × 5 days. Brackets first: 10 − 8 = 2. Then 2 × 4 × 5 = **40 hours a week**. Dollars: 40 × $45 = **$1,800 a week** billed for work nobody did.

c. Who is affected: you (your signature is on it), the crew, the lead hand, your employer (its name and its contract), the client (who pays), the general contractor. Pressures, for example:

- Personal: wanting to keep the job and fit in, knowing that it is dishonest
- Company: the lead hand's request, "everyone does it", fear of losing your spot
- Outside: the client's money, the contract, and the chance the general contractor finds out and the company loses the work

d. A strong answer: you do not sign the false hours. You say something like: "I can't sign for hours we didn't work. My name goes on that sheet. I'll sign for the 8 hours a day we did." If the lead hand pushes, you take it to the supervisor or the company office. Accept answers that refuse to sign false hours and name a calm next step.

## Task 3 (level 2)

a. Section 10.8 says a personal lock must only be removed by the worker who put it on. If that is not possible, the matter goes to the supervisor or manager in charge, who is responsible for removing it. Before removing it they must make every reasonable effort to contact the worker and make sure the machinery or equipment can be run safely. The worker must be told at the start of their next shift if their lock was removed.

b. **The rule has already decided it.** It is not your call to cut Jay's lock. The ethical part is only how you handle the pressure from the lead hand.

c. Example: "I can't cut Jay's lock. Under the lockout rules only Jay can take it off, or the supervisor has to try to reach him and check the saw is safe first. Can we call the supervisor now?" Next: the supervisor or manager in charge tries to reach Jay, checks the saw, and only then removes the lock, and Jay is told at the start of his next shift.

## Task 4 (level 3)

There is no single right answer, but a strong answer uses all four steps:

1. **Dilemma:** Priya wants the trip and her boss went last year, but taking a gift from a supplier while she is comparing its quote could bias her, or look like it does.
2. **Who is affected:** Priya, her boss, her company (which needs the best value), the three suppliers (who expect a fair comparison), the client (who pays in the end).
3. **Pressures:** Personal: a free weekend, not wanting to seem rude. Company: her boss went before, which makes it feel normal, and she may not know the policy. Outside: the supplier wants the order, and other suppliers expect fair treatment.
4. **Decide and say why:** for example, Priya declines while the quotes are open, checks the company's gift policy, and tells her boss about the invitation in writing. She picks the supplier on price, delivery and quality only. "Would I be comfortable if the other two suppliers knew?" is a good test here.

A response that accepts the trip should explain how Priya keeps the comparison fair (for example by asking her boss to make the final pick and putting the invitation on record). Mark the reasoning, not the choice.

## Task 5 (level 2)

a. Section 3.10 (report what appears to be unsafe as soon as possible), section 3.12 (do not carry out work you have reasonable cause to believe is an undue hazard, and report it right away) and section 3.13 (no prohibited action for refusing under 3.12).

b. Example: "I'm not comfortable working at that edge without the guardrail. Can we put it back before we carry on? It only takes a few minutes."

c. Under section 3.13 a worker must not be subject to prohibited action for refusing unsafe work under section 3.12. Section 47 of the Workers Compensation Act says prohibited action includes things like dismissal, suspension, discipline and intimidation.

## Task 6 (level 3)

Personal task. Points a good response covers:

- a real or realistic dilemma, named in one sentence
- the people affected
- at least one personal, one company and one outside pressure
- a decision, checked with at least one of the three questions
- one or more people the learner could ask for help (a supervisor, a safety representative, a union, an instructor, a mentor, or someone in the learner's own family or community they trust)
