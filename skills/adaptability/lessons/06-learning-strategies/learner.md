# Learning strategies that work

All people in this lesson are fictional.

## Why this matters on the job

A trade is learned over years: on the job, in technical training, from code books, manuals and drawings. Apprentices in BC write level exams and a certification exam that cover everything from all levels of training. How you study decides how much you still remember on exam day and on the job a year later. Some of the most popular study habits do very little. A few simple ones work much better.

## Key ideas

### What about learning styles?

You may have been told you are a "visual", "auditory" or "hands-on" learner, and that you learn best when teaching matches your style. Many people believe this. It has been tested.

- In 2008, a team of psychologists reviewed the research. They found no good evidence that matching teaching to a person's learning style improves learning. Some well-designed studies found the opposite.
- In a 2015 study, adults who said they were "auditory" learners did not learn more from an audiobook, and "visual" learners did not learn more from reading the same book.

People really do have preferences, and that is fine. But the best way to learn something depends mostly on **the thing being learned**. You learn a knot by tying it, a wiring diagram by looking at it, and the steps of a procedure by doing them and saying them. Good learners use all of these.

### Study methods ranked by evidence

A large 2013 review rated ten common study methods.

| Evidence | Method | What it looks like for an apprentice |
|---|---|---|
| Strong | **Practice testing** | Quiz yourself with flash cards or practice questions instead of rereading. |
| Strong | **Spaced practice** | Study a little on many days instead of all at once the night before. |
| Moderate | **Explaining steps** | Say out loud why each step of a calculation or procedure works. |
| Moderate | **Asking why** | Ask "why is this true?" about a fact or rule, and answer it. |
| Moderate | **Mixing problem types** | Mix area, volume and conversion questions in one practice set. |
| Weak | **Rereading** | Reading the same notes again. Feels good, does little. |
| Weak | **Highlighting** | Colouring the textbook. Feels good, does little. |

### Worked example

Jordan has two weeks before a Level 1 exam and about 6 hours to study.

- **Plan A:** reread the notes for 6 hours the night before.
- **Plan B:** 30 minutes a day for 12 days. Each session starts with 10 practice questions from memory, then checks the answers, then reviews only what was wrong. The topics are mixed.

Both plans use 6 hours (12 × 30 = 360 minutes). Plan B uses practice testing, spacing and mixing, so Jordan will remember far more on exam day.

## Practice

**Task 1** (level 1)
Mark each study method **S** (strong evidence), **M** (moderate) or **W** (weak).

1. Highlighting every important line in the textbook.
2. Answering practice exam questions, then checking the answers.
3. Studying 20 minutes a day for two weeks.
4. Explaining to a classmate why you add the overhang before cutting a rafter.
5. Reading your notes three times the night before the test.
6. Mixing fraction, metric conversion and area problems in one practice set.
7. Asking yourself why a rule in the safety manual exists.

**Task 2** (level 1)
Pick a topic from this program or your trade (for example, PPE, metric conversion, a tool, or a safety rule). Write five practice questions with the answers. Use them to quiz a partner.
