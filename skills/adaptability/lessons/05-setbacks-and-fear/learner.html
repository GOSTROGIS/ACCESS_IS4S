Setbacks, fear of failure and trying again

Use fear as a signal, turn a failed attempt into a plan, and know how exam rewrites work for BC apprentices.

  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 1-2
  • Time: 75 min
  • Components: Persist and persevere; Seek self-improvement; Regulate your emotions when appropriate

All people in this lesson are fictional.

Why this matters on the job

Every tradesperson has failed something: a weld test, a level exam, an interview, a first try at a stair layout. Apprentices in BC write level exams and a final certification exam, and not everyone passes the first time. What separates people who finish their apprenticeship from those who quit is often not talent. It is what they do in the week after a setback.

Key ideas

Fear can help or hold you back

Fear is a normal reaction to danger or to something new. An ACCESS lesson put it this way: healthy fear gets you moving, unhealthy fear freezes you.

  • Healthy fear: you are nervous about working at heights, so you check your harness and anchor twice.
  • Unhealthy fear: you are so afraid of failing the exam that you never register for it.

One label does not define you

"I'm stupid." "I'm bad at math." "I always mess up." A label like this is a whole judgement about you, and it gives you nothing to work on. Swap the label for something specific you can practise.

Label Specific
"I'm bad at math." "I haven't learned to change fractions to decimals yet."
"I'm useless with blueprints." "I mix up the plan view and the elevation view."

Believing you can improve, and doing the work

You may have heard of "growth mindset": the idea that skills grow with practice. That idea is true for trade skills. But the research on growth mindset lessons is more modest than the posters suggest. Large reviews have found that being told about growth mindset, on its own, makes little or no difference to most students' marks. One large study found a small benefit for lower-achieving students, mostly where the people around them backed the message up.

So believing you can get better is a good start, not the whole answer. What moves your results is specific practice, good study methods (lesson 6), feedback, and people who support you.

Five steps after a setback

  1. Feel it. Being disappointed or angry is normal. Give it a little time.
  2. Get the facts. What was the score? Which parts went well and which did not?
  3. Find one or two causes you can work on. For example, "I didn't practise with timed questions."
  4. Make a plan. Write what you will do, when, and who will help.
  5. Try again. Then check what changed.

Worked example. Priya is a first-year carpentry apprentice. On March 3 she writes her Level 1 exam and scores 64 percent. The pass mark is 70 percent.

  • Facts: she did well on safety and tools, and poorly on the math and drawings questions.
  • Cause she can work on: she studied by rereading her notes and never practised timed questions.
  • Plan: practise 30 minutes a day, five days a week, using practice questions. Meet a classmate on Saturdays to quiz each other. Ask her instructor which drawing topics to review.
  • Rewrite: SkilledTradesBC rewrites have a 30-day waiting period from the last attempt. Thirty days after March 3 is April 2, so April 2 is the earliest she can rewrite.

Exam rewrites for BC apprentices

SkilledTradesBC sets these rules for apprentices who score below 70 percent:

Attempt What you must do first Fee
2nd Schedule the rewrite through the SkilledTradesBC Portal. No fee
3rd Contact your Apprenticeship Advisor for guidance and approval. Certification exam $100. Level exam no fee.
4th Send proof of upgrading. Certification exam $100. Level exam no fee.
5th A member of the Completions Team contacts you for support first. Certification exam $100. Level exam no fee.

Check skilledtradesbc.ca/exam-rewrites before you book, because rules and fees can change.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Rewrite each label as something specific you could practise.

  1. "I'm hopeless at reading a tape measure."
  2. "I'm not a test person."
  3. "I'm too old to learn computers."
  4. "I always freeze up when the foreman watches me."

Task 2 (level 1) Mark each one H (healthy fear) or U (unhealthy fear).

  1. You are nervous about using a new saw, so you ask for a demonstration.
  2. You are worried about speaking at the toolbox talk, so you write two notes on a card.
  3. You are afraid of being laughed at, so you do not ask how the lockout works.
  4. You are scared of failing, so you do not register for technical training.
  5. You feel uneasy about a trench with no shoring, so you stay out and tell the foreman.

Task 3 (level 2) Use Priya's plan.

a. How many minutes will she practise in one week? b. She has 4 weeks before her rewrite. How many hours is that in total? c. Write one more thing she could add to her plan.

Task 4 (level 2) Make a fear action plan. Use a fear of your own, or use this one: "I'm afraid I'll fail my first job interview."

  1. The fear is: ____
  2. Who have I talked to about it? ____
  3. Who can be my buddy for this? ____
  4. What will I agree to do, and by when? ____
  5. What small step will I take each day this week? ____

Task 5 (level 2) Derek is an electrical apprentice. He has scored below 70 percent on his certification exam twice.

a. What must he do before he can book a third attempt? b. What will the third attempt cost? c. If he scores below 70 percent again, what must he send before a fourth attempt?

Check yourself

  1. What is the difference between healthy and unhealthy fear?
  2. Why is "I'm bad at math" less useful than "I haven't learned ratios yet"?
  3. What does research say about growth mindset lessons on their own?
  4. What are the five steps after a setback?
