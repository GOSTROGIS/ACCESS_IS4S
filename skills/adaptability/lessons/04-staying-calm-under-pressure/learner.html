Staying calm under pressure and handling anger

Notice anger early, cool down in ways that work, and say what you need without blowing up or bottling it up.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 1-2
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Regulate your emotions when appropriate; Demonstrate responsibility

All people and companies in this lesson are fictional.

Why this matters on the job

Job sites are full of pressure: deadlines, noise, heat, changing plans and people with strong opinions. Anger is a normal feeling, and it often tells you something matters. But anger near power tools, ladders, moving equipment or traffic can get someone hurt. People who stay calm under pressure, or calm down quickly after a flare-up, are the ones a crew trusts with responsibility.

Key ideas

Notice it early

Anger shows up in the body before it shows up in words. Common signs are a hot face, a fast heartbeat, tight jaw or fists, shallow breathing, a loud voice and racing thoughts. The earlier you notice, the more choices you have.

Anger styles

People learn different ways of dealing with anger from family, culture and past experience. This list comes from an ACCESS lesson.

Style What it looks like
Exploding Yelling, swearing, slamming, threatening.
Holding it in Saying nothing and letting it build.
Withholding Silent treatment, showing up late, "forgetting" a task for the person.
Body Headaches, stomach pain, poor sleep from anger that is not dealt with.
Turning it on yourself "It's all my fault. I'm useless."
Redirecting Taking it out on something or someone else, such as kicking a bin.
Channelling Turning the energy into getting a task done.
Assertive Saying calmly and directly what happened and what you need.

Assertive is usually the most useful style when the situation allows it.

What research says about cooling down

Many people believe that "letting off steam" by hitting something or going for a hard run gets rid of anger. A 2024 review of 154 studies found that activities that calm the body down (slow breathing, relaxing tight muscles, mindfulness, gentle yoga) reduced anger and aggression. Activities that rev the body up (hitting a bag, jogging, cycling) did not reduce anger on average. Exercise is good for your health, but it is not the best cool-down in the moment.

Cool-down options you can choose from:

  • If it is safe, put the tool down and step away. Say "I need five minutes."
  • Breathe out slowly, longer than you breathe in, a few times.
  • Unclench your jaw, drop your shoulders, open your hands.
  • Get a drink of water or some fresh air.
  • Talk it through later with someone you trust.

Use what works for you. Nobody has to do a breathing exercise to show they can handle pressure.

Then say what you need

Once you are calmer, an assertive statement has three parts:

"When [what happened], I [how it affected me]. I need [what you are asking for]."

Example: "When my tools went missing from the gang box, I lost half an hour. I need people to sign tools out on the sheet."

If you blew up, recover: own it the way you learned in lesson 2, and fix what you can.

Worked example: shouted at by the saw

Kyle is a first-year carpentry apprentice. At 10:00 a.m. the foreman walks past the saw station and shouts, in front of the crew: "What a mess! Somebody's going to break an ankle on these offcuts. Clean it up!"

1. Notice. Kyle's face goes hot and his hands close into fists. Part of the mess is his. Part is from the other crew. He wants to shout back, "It's not all mine!"

2. Step away from the argument, not the job. The saw is off. Kyle takes one slow breath out and says only: "Okay. I'll clean it up now."

3. Cool down. While he sweeps, he breathes out slowly, longer than he breathes in, and drops his shoulders. After a few minutes his heart rate is down.

4. Check the facts. The foreman's point was fair. Offcuts on the floor by a saw are a trip hazard. The way he said it, in front of the crew, is what stung.

5. Say what you need, at the right time. At break Kyle asks for a minute one-on-one:

"When you shouted at me about the saw station in front of the crew, I felt embarrassed and nearly snapped back. I'll clear my offcuts every hour from now on. I need you to tell me one-on-one next time."

Kyle owned his part, kept the crew safe and said what he needed. He did not blow up, and he did not bottle it up.

When the anger is coming at you

  • Keep your distance and keep your voice low and even.
  • Do not argue with someone who is shouting. Say you will talk when things are calmer.
  • If you feel unsafe, leave the area and tell your supervisor.

WorkSafeBC describes workplace bullying and harassment as actions that the person knew, or should have known, would humiliate or intimidate a worker. A supervisor taking reasonable action to manage and direct workers is not bullying. If you experience or see bullying and harassment, report it to your employer. If your employer does not take reasonable steps, you can call WorkSafeBC's Prevention Information Line at 604-276-3100 or toll-free 1-888-621-7233.

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) List three signs in your body that tell you anger is building. Circle the one you usually notice first.

Task 2 (level 1) Name the anger style in each situation.

  1. Trent slams his tool bag down and swears at the apprentice.
  2. Mia is angry at her foreman. She says nothing, then "forgets" to order the parts he asked for.
  3. Leo says nothing all week, but gets headaches every afternoon.
  4. After being yelled at, Ana tells herself, "I'm useless. I always mess up."
  5. At break, Raj tells the foreman, "When the plan changed without notice, I lost an hour. Can you text me changes?"
  6. After a bad call with the supplier, Jo kicks the recycling bin in the lunchroom.

Task 3 (level 2) Rewrite each outburst as an assertive statement using "When , I . I need ___."

  1. "Who keeps taking my tape? I'm sick of you guys!"
  2. "This schedule is garbage. Nobody here knows what they're doing."
  3. "Forget it. I'll just do everything myself."

Task 4 (level 2) Make your own cool-down plan. This is for you. You do not have to share it.

  • My earliest sign: ____
  • One calming action I will try: ____
  • What I will say to step away: ____
  • Who I can talk to afterwards: ____

Task 5 (level 2) Is each one reasonable direction or possible bullying and harassment? What could the worker do?

  1. The foreman tells you to redo a cut that is the wrong length.
  2. A co-worker hides your tools every day and laughs about it with others.
  3. Your supervisor moves you to clean-up for the day because the crane is down.
  4. A journeyperson calls you names in front of the crew, again and again.
  5. The foreman gives you a written warning after you were late three times without calling.

Check yourself

  1. Why does it help to notice anger early?
  2. Which kind of activity reduced anger in the 2024 review: ones that calm the body or ones that rev it up?
  3. What are the three parts of an assertive statement?
  4. Who should you report workplace bullying and harassment to first?
