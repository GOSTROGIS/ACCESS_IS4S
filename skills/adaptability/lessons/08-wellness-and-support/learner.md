# Looking after yourself with wellness and support in BC

All people in this lesson are fictional.

## Why this matters on the job

Trades work is hard on the body and the mind: early starts, long commutes, heavy lifting, noise, pressure to keep up, and sometimes weeks away at camp. WorkSafeBC tells workers never to work while overly tired or under the influence of alcohol or drugs. Looking after yourself is not a luxury. It is part of showing up fit for work, and it is how people last long enough in a trade to get their ticket.

## Key ideas

### The basics

The Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for adults aged 18 to 64 recommend:

- 7 to 9 hours of good-quality sleep, with regular bed and wake-up times
- at least 150 minutes a week of moderate to vigorous activity
- muscle-strengthening activities at least twice a week

Signs that stress is building up include trouble sleeping, getting irritated quickly, headaches, pulling away from people, and drinking or using more than usual.

### A wholistic view of wellness

The First Nations Health Authority (FNHA) in BC shares the **First Nations Perspective on Health and Wellness**. It shows wellness as circles:

- In the centre is the individual. Wellness starts with each of us taking responsibility for our own health.
- The second circle is the **mental, emotional, spiritual and physical** sides of a healthy, balanced life.
- The third circle is the values that hold wellness up: **respect, wisdom, responsibility and relationships**.
- The outer circle is the people and places we come from: **Nations, family, community and land**.

FNHA says the model is a starting point for discussion. Each Nation has its own teachings about wellness and balance. If you want to learn the teachings of your own Nation, ask an Elder or knowledge keeper from your community, or ask ACCESS staff to help you connect.

### Boundaries: saying no without burning bridges

A boundary is a line you draw to protect your time, health or goals. Saying no is hard when you want to help, when it is family, or when it is your boss. A simple pattern:

1. **Say no clearly.** "I can't do that."
2. **Give a short reason, if you want to.** You do not owe a long explanation.
3. **Offer something else, if you can.** "I can help on Sunday instead."

Example: "I can't lend you the truck tonight. I start at 6:30 tomorrow and I need it. I can drive you Saturday."

### Your worth is not for sale

An ACCESS lesson asked: can money buy self-esteem? Expensive trucks, boots and phones do not decide your worth. Keeping your word, learning your trade and treating people well build respect that lasts, including self-respect.

### Your support network

Everyone needs people to turn to. Think of three kinds of support:

- **Practical help:** a ride when your car breaks down, someone to watch the kids.
- **Someone to talk to:** a friend, relative, Elder, mentor or classmate.
- **Professional and community help:** counsellors, health services and help lines.

Have a backup plan for the things most likely to stop you getting to work, such as transportation and child care.

### BC supports

| If... | Call or contact |
|---|---|
| Someone is in danger right now, or there is a medical emergency | **9-1-1** |
| You or someone you know is thinking about suicide or is in crisis | Call or text **9-8-8** (Suicide Crisis Helpline), or call **1-800-784-2433** (1-800-SUICIDE), 24/7 |
| You are Indigenous and want culturally aware crisis support in BC | **KUU-US Crisis Line, 1-800-588-8717**, 24/7 |
| You are First Nations, Inuit or Métis and want to talk to a counsellor | **Hope for Wellness Helpline, 1-855-242-3310**, or online chat at hopeforwellness.ca, 24/7 |
| You need emotional support or mental health information, not an emergency | **310-6789** (no area code), 24/7 |
| You want health advice from a nurse | **HealthLink BC, 8-1-1**, nurses 24/7 |
| You want same-day help for opioid use | **Opioid Treatment Access Line, 1-833-804-8111**, 9 am to 4 pm |

More supports are listed at helpstartshere.gov.bc.ca.
