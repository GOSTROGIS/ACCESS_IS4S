# Working for yourself in the trades

All people and companies in this lesson are fictional. The tax, registration and WorkSafeBC facts are real and were checked on government websites in November 2026. Rates and limits change, so check them again before you use them.

## Why this matters on the job

Many tradespeople end up working for themselves at some point: a mobile welder with one truck, a finishing carpenter doing renovations, a millwright who takes contract work between plant jobs. Being your own boss means you set your hours and keep the profit. It also means nobody takes tax off your pay, nobody registers you with WorkSafeBC, and nobody sends your invoices for you. People who plan for that from day one do well. People who find out at tax time can owe thousands of dollars they have already spent.

Most people who work for themselves in a trade are already certified. As an apprentice, your hours count only when your sponsor reports them (lesson 12), so this lesson is about a later step on your path.

## Key ideas

### Employee or self-employed?

Reading lesson 22 ([Employee, apprentice or contractor?](../../../reading/lessons/22-employee-apprentice-or-contractor/learner.html)) goes through the full CRA test. This is the short version.

The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) looks at the **whole working relationship**, not just what people call it. It asks questions like these:

| Question | Points to employee | Points to self-employed |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls how and when the work is done? | The payer | You |
| Who supplies the tools and equipment? | The payer | You, and you pay for repairs and insurance |
| Can you hire a helper or send someone else? | No | Yes |
| Can you make a profit or take a loss on a job? | No, you get paid either way | Yes |

Being told "you're a contractor now, no paperwork" does not make it so. If the boss sets your hours, supplies everything and pays you by the hour, the facts may point to you being an employee.

### Set up the business

| Step | Where | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| **Choose a structure** | Your own decision, with advice | The three common types in BC are a **sole proprietorship** (one owner), a **general partnership** (two or more owners) and a **corporation** (owned by shareholders). A sole proprietor is self-employed and takes on all the business's debts and risks. The Province suggests getting advice from a lawyer or accountant. |
| **Register the name** | BC Registries | If you do business under **your own legal name only**, you do not need to register with the Province. If you use any other name, such as "Northside Mobile Welding", you need an approved name request and then you register the business. Registration cost $40 when checked. |
| **Get a business licence** | Each city or town where you work | Business licences come from local governments, not the Province. Some regions have an inter-municipal business licence for trades contractors that covers several cities. BizPaL (a free government tool) lists the permits and licences your business may need. |

### GST: the $30,000 test

You are a **small supplier** while your taxable sales are $30,000 or less in a single calendar quarter **and** over the last four calendar quarters in a row. A small supplier does not have to register for GST. You **must** register once you go over:

- **Over $30,000 in one quarter:** you stop being a small supplier right away and must charge GST on the sale that put you over.
- **Over $30,000 across four quarters in a row** (but not in one quarter): you stop being a small supplier at the end of the month after the quarter you went over. Your registration starts no later than your first sale after that, and you must register within 29 days of that sale.

In BC the GST rate is **5%**. The GST you collect is not your money. You send it to the CRA when you file your GST return. You can also register before you reach $30,000 if you choose. If you sell or lease taxable goods or services in BC, you may also need to register to collect Provincial Sales Tax (PST). The Province explains when.

### Income tax and CPP

When you work for yourself, nobody takes income tax or Canada Pension Plan (CPP) contributions off your pay. You pay them yourself:

- **CPP:** a self-employed person pays **both** the employee and the employer share. For 2026 each share is 5.95%, so you pay **11.9%** of your net self-employment earnings between $3,500 and $74,600. (Above that, a second contribution applies. This lesson stays below it.)
- **Instalments:** the CRA may ask you to pay income tax and CPP in instalments during the year instead of all at once.

The safe habit: **set money aside from every payment** in a separate account, so it is there when the tax is due. How much depends on your income and expenses. Work out your amount with an accountant.

### WorkSafeBC

- As a sole proprietor or partner, you are **not automatically covered** if you are hurt at work. You can buy **Personal Coverage** (it used to be called Personal Optional Protection) for yourself.
- **If you hire workers, you must register** with WorkSafeBC.
- General contractors ask subcontractors for a **clearance letter**. If a GC hires a registered sub who is not paying WorkSafeBC premiums, the GC can be held liable for those premiums. A clearance letter shows your account is in good standing.

## Worked example: Dana's first year

Dana is a Red Seal welder. She starts a one-truck mobile welding business as a sole proprietor called Northside Mobile Welding.

**Setting up.** Because "Northside Mobile Welding" is not her own name, she gets a name approved and registers the business with BC Registries. She gets a business licence where she lives and checks the licence rules in each city she works in. She buys Personal Coverage from WorkSafeBC. A GC she wants to work for asks for a clearance letter and proof of liability insurance before her first job, so she gets both.

**The GST test.** Dana's taxable sales by quarter:

| Quarter | Sales |
|---|---|
| January to March | $6,800 |
| April to June | $9,400 |
| July to September | $8,900 |
| October to December | $7,600 |
| **Four quarters** | 6,800 + 9,400 + 8,900 + 7,600 = **$32,700** |

No single quarter is over $30,000. But the four quarters together are over $30,000. Dana stops being a small supplier at the end of January, the month after the October to December quarter, starts charging GST on the first sale after that, and must register within 29 days of that sale.

**An invoice with GST.** After she registers, Dana does a job: $2,400 labour and $600 materials.

1. Subtotal: $2,400 + $600 = $3,000
2. GST: 5% of $3,000 = 0.05 × $3,000 = $150
3. Total to the customer: $3,000 + $150 = **$3,150**

**Setting money aside.** Dana and her accountant agreed on a plan: keep all the GST, plus 25% of each subtotal for income tax and CPP. The 25% is Dana's own plan, not a rule.

Set aside: $150 + 25% of $3,000 = $150 + $750 = **$900**.

**CPP at year end.** In 2026 Dana's net self-employment earnings are $52,000. Using BEDMAS, brackets first:

CPP = ($52,000 − $3,500) × 11.9% = $48,500 × 0.119 = **$5,771.50**

That is money an employer would have paid half of. Now Dana pays all of it.
