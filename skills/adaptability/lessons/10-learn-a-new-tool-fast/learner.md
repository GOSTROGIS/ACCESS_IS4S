# Learn a new tool or method fast

All people, companies and products in this lesson are fictional, except where a source is named.

## Why this matters on the job

Every few months you will meet a tool or method you have never used: a new press tool, a laser level, a different torque wrench, a new app for timesheets. Some apprentices fake it and hope nobody notices. Some freeze and wait to be shown. The ones who move ahead have a simple way to learn a new tool quickly and safely, and they ask for help in the right way.

1. **Read.** Start with the manual. Read the safety section first, then the parts and the steps. Reading lesson 8 shows you how to find things in a manual fast.
2. **Watch.** Ask a qualified person to show you. Watch their hands, and ask why they do each step.
3. **Try.** Do it yourself, slowly, on scrap or a practice piece, with someone nearby.
4. **Check.** Compare your result with the spec or the manual. Ask for feedback. Then go round the loop again.

Speed comes last. First get it right every time. Then get faster.

### What BC law says about training

- Under the BC Occupational Health and Safety Regulation (section 4.3), the employer must make sure each tool is used and operated according to the manufacturer's instructions, if available, safe work practices and the Regulation.
- Before a young or new worker starts work, the employer must give an orientation that includes instruction and demonstration of the worker's work task or work process (section 3.23).
- If a young or new worker asks for more training, or cannot do the task safely, the employer must provide more orientation and training (section 3.24). A young worker is anyone under 25. A new worker includes anyone new to the workplace, and anyone whose workplace hazards have changed or who moves to a workplace with different hazards.

So asking "Can you show me before I use it?" is not a weakness. It is your right, and it is what the law expects.

### Keep a practice log

A practice log is a short record of each try: the time it took and whether it passed the check. It shows you, and your supervisor, when you are ready to work alone. Two things matter:

- **Pass rate:** how many tries passed out of how many tries.
- **Time:** how long each try took.

A fast try that fails the check does not count. Quality first.

### Good questions to ask during a demonstration

- "What is the most common mistake with this tool?"
- "How do you check that it is done right?"
- "What do you do if it stops halfway?"
- "Where is the manual kept?"

- Section 1 (page 2): safety warnings. Keep fingers out of the jaws. Wear safety glasses.
- Section 3 (page 6): pick the jaw that matches the pipe size stamped on the jaw.
- Section 4 (page 8): cut the pipe square, deburr it, mark the insertion depth, push the pipe into the fitting up to the mark, then press.
- Section 5 (page 11): check that the mark sits at the edge of the fitting after the press.

**Watch.** The journeyperson, Ray, makes two joints while Dana watches. She asks, "What's the most common mistake?" Ray says, "Not pushing the pipe all the way in. That's why you mark it."

**Try.** Dana makes five practice joints on scrap pipe at the bench. Ray stays close by.

**Check.** After each joint she checks the insertion mark and writes the time and the result in her log.

Try 1 failed the check. The mark showed a gap: the pipe was not all the way into the fitting. Dana marked the next pipes more carefully and pushed them home before pressing. Tries 2 to 5 all passed.

Her numbers:

- Pass rate: 4 passes out of 5 tries.
- Average time of the four passing tries: (4.5 + 4.0 + 3.5 + 3.0) ÷ 4 = 15.0 ÷ 4 = 3.75 minutes.
- Drop in time from try 1 to try 5: 6.0 − 3.0 = 3.0 minutes. As a percent of the first time: 3.0 ÷ 6.0 × 100 = 50%.

Dana tells Ray: "Four passes in a row now. I'm at about 3 minutes a joint. You're at 2. Can I do the next ten on the job with you checking each one?" That is a clear, honest ask.
