Owning your work and your mistakes

Report mistakes early, focus on what you control, and answer criticism without blaming or caving in.

All people and companies in this lesson are fictional.

Why this matters on the job

Everyone on a crew makes mistakes. A sleeve in the wrong spot or a mislabelled breaker costs little to fix today and a lot to fix after the concrete is poured or the inspector arrives. Foremen trust the apprentice who speaks up early, not the one who never seems to make mistakes. In BC, reporting an unsafe condition or act is also the law.

Key ideas

What accountability looks like

Being accountable means you:

  • do what you said you would do, or say early that you cannot
  • tell the truth about where your work is at, including what is not done
  • report mistakes and hazards as soon as you see them
  • fix what is yours to fix and learn from it

It does not mean taking the blame for things that were not yours, or saying sorry for everything.

What the law says about reporting

Under BC's Occupational Health and Safety Regulation (section 3.10), anyone who sees what looks like an unsafe or harmful condition or act must report it to a supervisor or the employer as soon as possible. If you are hurt at work, WorkSafeBC says to report it to your employer as soon as possible. Employers are not allowed to punish workers for raising a health and safety issue.

Control, influence and no control

Put your energy where it can make a difference.

You control You can influence You cannot control
Your actions and words What your crew decides The weather
When you leave home How your foreman sees you Other people's moods
How you answer criticism The order of tasks, if you ask A supplier's delivery truck

Reporting a mistake in four parts

  1. What happened. Plain facts, no excuses.
  2. What I have done. Any step you already took to make it safe or stop it getting worse.
  3. What I need. A decision, help or materials.
  4. What I will do next time. One specific change.

Worked example. Marcus is a first-year plumbing apprentice at Salal Creek Mechanical Ltd. At 4:30 p.m. he sees that he set out the floor sleeves on Level 2 from revision B of the drawing. Revision C moved two sleeves. The slab pour is booked for 7:00 a.m. tomorrow. He calls his foreman, Angela, right away:

"Angela, it's Marcus. I set the Level 2 sleeves from revision B. Revision C moved sleeves 4 and 7 about a metre east. I've flagged both with orange tape and haven't moved anything yet. I need to know if I should reset them tonight or first thing at 6. From now on I'll check the revision letter on the drawing before I lay out."

Marcus did not blame the drawing office or say "it's not a big deal". He gave Angela what she needed to decide before the pour.

Worked example: six studs cut short

Jesse is a first-year carpentry apprentice at Riverbend Framing Ltd. The cut list for the Unit 2 kneewall calls for 6 studs at 1200 mm. At 10:30 a.m. Jesse checks the first stud against the wall and sees a gap. He measures all 6. They are 1180 mm, 20 mm short. He read 1180 from the line below on the cut list.

Weak report. "Uh, a few studs came out short. The tape must be off. Not a big deal." This does not say how many, how short, or what Jesse did. It blames the tape. The foreman has to dig for the facts.

Strong report, step by step.

  1. What happened. "I cut the 6 kneewall studs for Unit 2 at 1180 instead of 1200. I read the wrong line on the cut list."
  2. What I have done. "I've set them aside, marked them SHORT and haven't nailed any in."
  3. What I need. "I need 6 more studs from the stack. Do you want the short ones used as blocking?"
  4. What I will do next time. "I'll check the first cut against the cut list and the wall before I cut the rest."

Why speak up now? If the short studs were nailed in, the kneewall would sit 20 mm low and the rafters would not bear on it properly. Fixing it now takes a few minutes. Fixing it after the rafters are on could take hours. The 1180 mm pieces are still useful as blocking, so nothing is wasted.

Answering criticism: the A B C D tools

These four tools come from an ACCESS lesson. Use them alone or together.

  • Acknowledge: accept a fair point without a long apology. "You're right, I left the gang box open. I'll lock it before I leave."
  • Boundaries: say how you expect to be treated. "I'd like to talk about this away from the crew. And please drop the name-calling."
  • Clarify: ask for details so you can fix the real problem. "What part of the cut looks wrong to you?"
  • Disagree: calmly correct something untrue. "I disagree. I've been on time every day this month. If there's a problem with today, let's talk about it."

Practice

Task 1 (level 1) Mark each statement O (owning it) or E (excuse or blame).

  1. "The drawing was confusing, so it's not my fault."
  2. "I measured from the wrong gridline. I'll recheck the rest before I cut."
  3. "Nobody told me the pour moved to Thursday."
  4. "I missed the start of the toolbox talk. Can you give me the hazards I missed?"
  5. "Everyone else leaves their tools out too."

Task 2 (level 1) Sort these into control, influence or no control: the bus being late, what time you get up, your foreman's mood, the questions you ask in class, the date of your Level 1 exam, whether you study tonight, what a co-worker says about you, whether you report a frayed extension cord.

Task 3 (level 2) Sam, an electrical apprentice at Coastline Electrical Contractors, finds out at 3:00 p.m. that two circuits on the panel directory in Suite 204 are labelled the wrong way round. The inspector comes tomorrow at 9:00 a.m. Write what Sam should say to the foreman, using the four parts.

Task 4 (level 2) Draw a ripple: a small circle in the middle and three rings around it. In the centre, write a decision, such as "skip Friday's class to work an extra paid shift", or one of your own. In ring 1, write how it affects you (good and bad). In ring 2, the people closest to you. In ring 3, others such as classmates, your sponsor or your crew. You do not have to share a personal decision.

Task 5 (level 2) With a partner, choose a tool (A, B, C or D) for each statement and practise a reply. Write your best reply for one of them.

  1. "You left the gang box open again. Do you even care about this job?"
  2. "This work is sloppy."
  3. "You're always late." (You have been on time all month.)
  4. "Are you stupid? Anyone could do that cut." (Said in front of the crew.)

Check yourself

  1. Under BC law, what must you do when you see what looks like an unsafe condition?
  2. What are the four parts of reporting a mistake?
  3. Name two things that are in your control at work.
  4. Which A B C D tool helps you find out what the real problem is?
