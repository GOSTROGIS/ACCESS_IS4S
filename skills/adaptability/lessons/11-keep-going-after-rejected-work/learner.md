# Keep going after rejected work

All people, companies and places in this lesson are fictional. The BC Building Code limits are real.

## Why this matters on the job

Sooner or later an inspector will fail something you built, or a test will fail on work you did: a stair, a weld, a pressure test, a rough-in. It stings. Some people argue with the inspector, blame the drawings or go quiet for the rest of the day. That makes things worse. The crews that do well treat a rejection as a list of things to fix. They fix it right, check it themselves, and pass the second time.

## Key ideas

### What a rejection is, and what it is not

A **notice of deficiency** (or a failed test) says one thing: this work does not meet the rule yet. It is about the work, not about you as a person. Read it as a to-do list.

### Six steps after rejected work

1. **Cool down.** Use what you learned in lesson 4. Do not argue on site. Say "Thanks. I'll read this and get back to you."
2. **Read it all.** What failed? Which rule? What does it ask you to do?
3. **Report it.** Tell your foreman, using the four parts from lesson 2.
4. **Find the real cause.** Measure. Ask "why?" until you reach something you can fix.
5. **Plan the fix and the re-inspection.** What, who, when, and how you will check it yourself first.
6. **Add a check.** Change your routine so the same thing does not happen again.

### Keep trying or change the approach?

Lesson 3 asked this question. It matters even more after a fail.

- If the same fix has failed twice, **change the approach** or **ask for help**. A third try of the same thing usually fails the same way.
- If each try is closer, **keep going**.
- If it is unsafe, **stop and report**.

### The stair rules in this lesson

The BC Building Code 2024 sets limits for stairs. For a **private stair** (a stair that serves a single dwelling unit, such as the stair inside a house or townhouse):

- **Rise** is measured from the top of one tread to the top of the next (nosing to nosing). Each rise must be no more than **200 mm** and no less than **125 mm** (Table 9.8.4.1).
- Risers in one flight must be uniform. Two risers next to each other can differ by no more than **5 mm**. The tallest and shortest riser in the flight can differ by no more than **10 mm** (Sentence 9.8.4.4.(1)).

Lay out a stair from **finished floor to finished floor**. Flooring changes the top and bottom risers.

Tom, the apprentice who built the stair, feels his face get hot. He wants to say "It was fine on Friday." Instead he says, "Thanks. I'll go through it with my foreman." Then he follows the steps.

**Read it all.** The stair failed. The rule is Sentence 9.8.4.4.(1) and Table 9.8.4.1. The inspector wants it fixed and a re-inspection booked.

**Find the real cause.** Tom measures every riser, nosing to nosing, with the finished floors in.

He checks each rule.

| Check | Rule | Unit 4 stair | Pass? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottom pair of risers | differ by 5 mm or less | 190 − 184 = 6 | no |
| Top pair of risers | differ by 5 mm or less | 209 − 190 = 19 | no |
| Middle risers | differ by 5 mm or less | at most 191 − 189 = 2 | yes |
| Tallest minus shortest in the flight | 10 mm or less | 209 − 184 = 25 | no |
| Largest rise, private stair | 200 mm or less | 209 | no |

Why did this happen? The stringers were cut for 14 equal risers of 190 mm, measured from subfloor to subfloor: 14 × 190 = 2660 mm. Then 6 mm vinyl went on the main floor and 19 mm hardwood went on the upper floor.

- The bottom riser lost the vinyl thickness: 190 − 6 = 184 mm.
- The top riser gained the hardwood thickness: 190 + 19 = 209 mm.

The real cause is the layout method, not bad cutting.

**Report it.** Tom calls his foreman, Dana:

> "Dana, it's Tom. The Unit 4 stair failed. I laid it out subfloor to subfloor, so the vinyl and hardwood threw off the top and bottom risers. The top one is 209. I've taped off the stair so nobody uses it until it's fixed. I need new stringers and about a day. From now on I'll lay out from finished floor heights."

**Plan the fix.** The total finished rise is 2660 − 6 + 19 = 2673 mm. Check it against the measured risers: they add up to 2673 mm too. With 14 risers, each rise is 2673 ÷ 14 = 190.9 mm, about 191 mm. That is under 200 mm. To hit 2673 mm exactly, Tom lays out 13 risers at 191 mm and one at 190 mm: 13 × 191 + 190 = 2483 + 190 = 2673 mm. Next to each other they differ by only 1 mm.

**Plan the re-inspection.** Re-inspections are booked 2 business days ahead.
