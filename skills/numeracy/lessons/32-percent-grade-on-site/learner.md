# Percent grade between two points on a site

## Why this matters on the job

Driveways, sidewalks, ramps, drain pipes and the ground around a building all slope on purpose. The slope moves water away and lets people and machines move safely. A drawing gives the slope as a grade, such as 2%, or as "1 in 12". On site you work out the grade from two elevations and a distance, or work out how much fall a pipe needs, and check it against the drawing or the code.

## Key ideas

**Grade compares rise with run.** The rise is the difference in elevation. The run is the **horizontal** distance between the points.

- grade (%) = rise ÷ run × 100
- 1 in n: n = run ÷ rise
- rise = run × grade (%) ÷ 100
- angle in degrees = tan⁻¹(rise ÷ run), with your calculator in degree mode

Use BEDMAS. Find the difference in elevation first, then divide, then multiply by 100. Keep all the digits until the last step, then round.

**From elevations.** A point at 48.920 and a point at 48.380 are 27.0 m apart. Rise = 48.920 − 48.380 = 0.540 m. Grade = 0.540 ÷ 27.0 × 100 = 2%. Water runs from the higher point to the lower point, so say which way the grade falls.

**Percent and "1 in" are two ways to write the same slope.** You met "1 in n" for roofs in [lesson 16](../16-slope-pitch-and-stairs/learner.html).

- % to 1 in n: n = 100 ÷ percent. 5% is 1 in 20.
- 1 in n to %: percent = 100 ÷ n. 1 in 12 is 8.33%.
- A bigger percent, or a smaller n, is steeper.

**Percent is not degrees.** A 100% grade rises 1 m for every 1 m of run. That is 45°, not 100°. A 10% grade is only 5.71°.

**Horizontal, not along the ground.** Grade uses the horizontal distance. A tape laid along a gentle slope reads only a little more. A drive with a rise of 1.200 m that measures 16.045 m along the ground is √(16.045² − 1.200²) = 16.000 m horizontally (lesson 15).

**What the BC Building Code 2024 says about ramps and grading** (Division B):

| What | Limit | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Exterior ramp, Part 9 building | not more than 1 in 10 | Article 9.8.5.4 |
| Interior ramp serving a residential occupancy, Part 9 | not more than 1 in 10 | Article 9.8.5.4 |
| Ramp in an industrial occupancy, Part 9 | not more than 1 in 6 | Article 9.8.5.4 |
| Ramp in any other occupancy, Part 9 | not more than 1 in 8 | Article 9.8.5.4 |
| Ramp in an accessible path of travel | not more than 1 in 12 | Clause 3.8.3.5.(1)(b) |
| Accessible path of travel steeper than 1 in 20 | must be designed as a ramp | Clause 3.8.3.2.(3)(g) |
| Ground around a Part 9 building | graded "so that water will not accumulate at or near the building" | Article 9.14.6.1 |

Article 9.8.5.4 covers pedestrian ramps that are not in an accessible path of travel. Ramps in an accessible path follow Article 3.8.3.5 (Article 9.8.5.1).

### Worked example

A garage slab is at 101.350. The curb at the street is at 100.150. The horizontal distance is 16.0 m.

1. Rise: 101.350 − 100.150 = **1.200 m**
2. Grade: 1.200 ÷ 16.0 × 100 = **7.5%**, falling toward the street
3. 1 in n: 16.0 ÷ 1.200 = 13.33, so **1 in 13.33**
4. Angle: tan⁻¹(1.200 ÷ 16.0) = **4.29°**

### Areas of lots: hectares and acres

Land is often listed in hectares (ha) or in acres.

- 1 ha = 10,000 m². A lot 100 m by 100 m is 1 ha.
- 1 acre = 4,840 square yards = 4,046.856 m² (to three decimals).
- m² to ha: divide by 10,000. ha to acres: multiply by 10,000, then divide by 4,046.856.
