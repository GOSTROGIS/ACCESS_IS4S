# Scrap, rework and control charts

**Session:** Numeracy lesson 30 of 34. **Total length:** 90 minutes.

**Before the session:** learners need a calculator. Print or project the two control charts on the learner page. If the shop is free, bring 5 offcuts of the same nominal length and a tape so learners can measure a real sample and work out its average and range in step 2.1. Tasks 1 to 4 are level 2 and suit every learner. Task 5 (working out control limits) is level 3. The factors 0.577 and 2.114 are given on the learner page, so learners do not need the full table. Do not add machining tolerances or drawing limits to any example. The lesson uses cut list lengths only, and teaches that control limits and drawing limits are different things. Every company, part count and cost is made up for practice.

## Opening (5 min)

Welcome learners. Hold up an offcut or a bent bracket and ask: "What did this cost the company?" Most learners say the price of the steel. Ask: "Who cut it? Who welded it? Who will cut the next one?" Say: "Scrap is paid for twice. Today we put a number on scrap and rework, and we learn a chart that warns you before a saw or a jig starts making bad parts." Tell learners this is session 30 of the Numeracy set, and it runs 90 minutes.

## Objective (2 min)

By the end of the session, learners will be able to:

- recognize when scrap, rework or a drifting process needs numbers to judge it (Identify the task that will require you to use numeracy)
- pick out parts made, scrapped, reworked, labour hours and costs, and read the centre line and limits of a control chart (Identify the mathematical information)
- connect scrap and rework to productivity and to job cost (Make connections between related pieces of mathematical information)
- work out scrap rate, first-pass yield, productivity, parts to start and, at level 3, control limits (Apply mathematical operations and tools you will need to answer the question)
- tell normal variation from a signal on a control chart (Interpret and evaluate the information)
- report a signal and the action taken in two clear sentences (Share the mathematical information, results and implications)

## Pre-assessment (3 min)

1. What is 12 out of 240 as a percent? (5%)
2. Find the average of 4, 6, 5 and 9. (24 ÷ 4 = 6)

## Post-assessment (5 min)

1. A shop made 500 parts and scrapped 15. What is the scrap rate? (3%)
2. 96 good parts are needed and the scrap rate is 4%. How many should be started? (96 ÷ 0.96 = 100)
3. On an X-bar chart, every point is inside the limits, but the last 8 are all below the centre line. What do you do? (Treat it as a signal and look for a cause)

## Summary (5 min)

During this session we covered:

- scrap rate and first-pass yield
- how many parts to start, rounded up
- labour productivity and the cost of scrap and rework
- reading an X-bar chart: normal variation, a point outside a limit, and a run of 8
- why control limits are not the drawing's limits, and why a stable process is left alone
- working out the centre line and control limits from samples of 5

## Sources

- NIST/SEMATECH *e-Handbook of Statistical Methods*, "6.3.2. What are Variables Control Charts?", https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc32.htm, checked 2026-11-08: the Western Electric rules include "Any Point Above +3 Sigma" and "8 Consecutive Points on This Side of Control Line", and a point outside ±3 sigma is "a rare event", so "we conclude the process has shifted".
- NIST/SEMATECH *e-Handbook of Statistical Methods*, "6.3.2.1. Shewhart X-bar and R and S Control Charts", https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc321.htm, checked 2026-11-08: control charts use "the customary 3-sigma control limits", the range method suits small samples, and "A typical sample size is 4 or 5".
- Oregon State University Extension, *Statistical Process Control, Part 7: Variables Control Charts* (EM 9109), https://extension.oregonstate.edu/sites/extd8/files/documents/em9109.pdf, checked 2026-11-08: the X-bar chart plots sample averages with the overall average as the centre line, the range is the largest value minus the smallest, the limits use A2 × R̄ for the X-bar chart and D3 and D4 for the R chart, Table 6 gives A2 = 0.577, D3 = 0 and D4 = 2.114 for a sample of 5, and "there is no lower control limit for the R chart when the sample size is six or fewer". The same publication lists other versions of the run rule (7 points in a row). This lesson uses the NIST version of 8.
- Yonatan Reshef, University of Alberta, "Deming's funnel experiment", https://sites.ualberta.ca/~yreshef/orga432/funnel.html, checked 2026-11-08: "tampering invariably increases variation in the results of a stable process", and "specification limits are not action limits". This supports the lesson's advice to leave a stable process alone and to keep drawing limits separate from control limits.
- eCampusOntario, *Fundamentals of Operations Management*, "2.4 The Power of Productivity Measurement", https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/fundamentalsopsmgmt/chapter/2-4-the-power-of-productivity-measurement-gauging-operational-performance, checked 2026-11-08: labour productivity "measures the output (goods or services produced) per unit of labour input (typically labour hours)".
- The scrap, rework and productivity method (turning a scrap count into lost good parts, lost productivity and cost) follows the structure of the instructor's own operations management coursework. All companies, part counts, costs, lengths and chart data in this lesson are new and made up for practice.
- Every rate, yield, productivity, cost, average, range and control limit was computed with a Python script and checked. The two charts were drawn by script from those numbers.
