Setting heights with a builder's level

Carry an elevation from a benchmark with backsights and foresights, fill in a level book and check it, and work out the rod reading, cut or fill for a footing, a slab or a pipe.

  • Learner
  • Instructor🔒 (instructor code)
  • Answers🔒 (instructor code)
  • Level: 2-3
  • Time: 90 min
  • Components: Identify the mathematical information; Make connections between related pieces of mathematical information; Apply mathematical operations and tools you will need to answer the question; Interpret and evaluate the information; Share the mathematical information, results and implications

Session: Numeracy lesson 31 of 34. Total length: 90 minutes.

Before the session: learners need a calculator. If the shop has a builder's level or an automatic level, a tripod and a rod, set them up before the session for the demonstration in step 3.2. A tape measure and a few stakes are enough if there is no level: learners can then read a rod held against a wall and a string line. Learners should be sure of decimals to three places (lesson 11) and BEDMAS (lesson 12). Lesson 32 uses the elevations from this lesson to work out grade.

Opening (5 min)

Welcome learners. Ask: "The drawing says the top of the footing is at 99.700. How do you know where 99.700 is on a bare lot?" Take answers. Say: "Every height on a site comes from one point that does not move, the benchmark. Today you carry that height to the work with a level and a rod, write it in a level book, and check your own sums before anyone pours concrete." Tell learners this is session 31 of the Numeracy set, and it runs 90 minutes.

Objective (2 min)

By the end of the session, learners will be able to:

  • pick out the benchmark elevation, backsights, foresights and design elevations from a level book or a job description (Identify the mathematical information)
  • connect a rod reading to a height: a big reading is a low point, a small reading is a high point (Make connections between related pieces of mathematical information)
  • work out the height of instrument, elevations, rod readings for a design height, and cut or fill (Apply mathematical operations and tools you will need to answer the question)
  • check a level book with the arithmetic check and find a mistake (Interpret and evaluate the information)
  • report a mistake and a corrected elevation to a foreman (Share the mathematical information, results and implications)

Pre-assessment (3 min)

  1. 100.000 + 1.425 − 1.875 = ? (99.550)
  2. Which is higher, a point at 99.550 or a point at 99.415? (99.550)

Learning tasks (70 min)

Step Instructor activities Learner activities Resources Time
1.1 Teach benchmark, backsight, HI and foresight with a sketch of a level between a pole and a pad. Walk through the worked example, steps 1 to 5. Stress: backsight added, foresight subtracted. 1.1 Follow the worked example and say each step back. Learner page, whiteboard 12 min
1.2 Assign Task 1. Ask: "Which point has the biggest rod reading? Is it high or low?" 1.2 Complete Task 1. Learner page 8 min
1.3 Teach "rod reading needed = HI − design elevation" with step 6 of the worked example. Model Task 2a. 1.3 Complete Task 2. Learner page 10 min
2.1 Teach turning points and the level book. Show how the elevation carries from one setup to the next. Teach the arithmetic check and do it for BM to B in the worked example: exclude A, which is an intermediate sight. 1.425 − 2.010 = 99.415 − 100.000 = −0.585. 2.1 Complete Task 3, including the check. Learner page 14 min
2.2 Teach cut and fill: ground minus design. Positive is cut, negative is fill. 2.2 Complete Task 4. Learner page, Hub Construction Operations 10 min
3.1 Assign Task 5 in pairs. Circulate. Ask: "The check is out by exactly 0.100. What kind of mistake gives a round number like that?" Read the notes to the foreman. 3.1 Complete Task 5, including the note. Learner page 12 min
3.2 If there is a level, set up between two points, take a backsight on a "benchmark" with an agreed elevation and let pairs read foresights and work out the elevations. Otherwise, pairs swap Task 3 tables and check each other's arithmetic check. 3.2 Read the rod and work out elevations, or check a partner's level book. Level, tripod and rod, or learner page 4 min

Post-assessment (5 min)

  1. A benchmark is at 20.000. The backsight is 1.550 and the foresight on a stake is 2.150. Find the elevation of the stake. (HI = 21.550, stake = 19.400)
  2. The HI is 21.550 and a footing bottom must be at 19.200. What should the rod read? (2.350)
  3. A level book starts at 50.000. The backsights add to 5.210 and the foresights add to 4.860. What must the last elevation be? (50.000 + 5.210 − 4.860 = 50.350)

Summary (5 min)

During this session we covered:

  • benchmark, backsight, height of instrument, foresight and turning point
  • HI = known elevation + BS, and elevation = HI − FS
  • the rod reading for a design height: HI − design elevation
  • cut and fill from ground minus design
  • the level book and its arithmetic check: sum of BS − sum of FS = last elevation − first elevation
  • reporting a mistake with the corrected number

Sources

  • U.S. Geological Survey, Kennedy, E. J., Levels at Streamflow Gaging Stations, Open-File Report 88-710, https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1988/0710/report.pdf, checked 2026-11-08: the backsight "is added to the point's known elevation to obtain the elevation of the instrument's line of sight or height of instrument (HI)", the foresight "is subtracted from the height of instrument, and the result is the elevation of the point under the rod", the equations "Elev. + BS = HI, and HI - FS = Elev.", and errors are reduced "by balancing the length of the backsight and foresight for each setup of the instrument".
  • Indiana Department of Transportation, Use and Care of Level, chapter 2, https://www.in.gov/indot/files/proced1chapter2.pdf, checked 2026-11-08: a backsight is "a rod reading taken at a point of known elevation, such as a benchmark or turning point", also called a plus sight. A foresight is a rod reading "taken on a point for which the elevation is to be established", also called a minus sight. A turning point is "required to be solid, stable, and not move or change in elevation" between set-ups.
  • Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, Fishpond Engineering: A Technical Manual for Small- and Medium-Scale Coastal Fish Farms in Southeast Asia, section 3.6.2, https://www.fao.org/4/e7171e/E7171E03.htm, checked 2026-11-08: the arithmetic check "Sum BS - Sum FS = Elev. BM 2 - Elev. BM 1", used "to check for the accuracy of addition and subtraction".
