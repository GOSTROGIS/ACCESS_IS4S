Will the job run out of cash

Lay out the money coming in and going out of a small crew month by month, find the month the bank balance is lowest, and work out how big a line of credit the crew needs.

Why this matters on the job

A small crew can win good work, price it well and still run out of money. The crew is paid every two weeks. The supplier wants its money in 30 days. The general contractor pays 30 days after the claim, keeps 10% as holdback, and pays that weeks after the job is done. A cash flow forecast shows, month by month, whether there will be enough money in the bank. Anyone who wants to run a crew one day needs to read one.

Key ideas

Cash flow is money in and money out of the bank, in the month it actually moves. It is not the same as profit. A job can make a profit over the season and still leave the bank account below zero for some months.

Money in comes when the customer pays, not when the work is done or billed.

Money out goes when the company pays, not when it buys or uses something.

For each month:

  1. total in = all the money in that month
  2. total out = all the money out that month
  3. net cash flow = total in − total out
  4. balance at end of month = balance at start + net cash flow

The balance at the end of one month is the balance at the start of the next.

What moves the timing on a construction job:

  • Progress claims and holdback (lesson 23). The general contractor keeps 10% of each claim as holdback, as the BC Builders Lien Act requires, and pays it after the holdback period. GST is paid on the claim less holdback.
  • Supplier terms. "Net 30" means the bill is due 30 days after the invoice.
  • Payroll remittances. The money an employer takes off pay for income tax, CPP and EI, plus the employer's own CPP and EI, goes to the Canada Revenue Agency. A regular remitter must have it to the CRA by the 15th of the month after the employees were paid.
  • GST. GST collected on claims belongs to the CRA. A company that files every quarter must file and pay no later than one month after the end of the quarter.

Line of credit. A bank lets the company go below zero up to a set limit, and charges interest on what it uses. The lowest balance in the forecast shows how big the limit needs to be. Most owners add a cushion for surprises.

Worked example

Kestrel Roofing Ltd. (a fictional company) is a two-person roofing crew. It starts April with $8,000.00 in the bank. In April it claims $24,000.00 of work from the general contractor. The contractor pays in May, 30 days later: it keeps 10% holdback and adds 5% GST to what it pays.

  1. Holdback: $24,000.00 × 0.10 = $2,400.00
  2. Paid now, before GST: $24,000.00 − $2,400.00 = $21,600.00
  3. GST: $21,600.00 × 0.05 = $1,080.00
  4. Money in, May: $21,600.00 + $1,080.00 = $22,680.00

In one step: $24,000.00 × 0.90 × 1.05 = $24,000.00 × 0.945 = $22,680.00.

April has no money in, because nothing is paid until May. April's money out is $11,400.00. So:

  1. April net cash flow: $0.00 − $11,400.00 = −$11,400.00
  2. Balance at end of April: $8,000.00 + (−$11,400.00) = −$3,400.00

Kestrel is already below zero after the first month, on a job that will make money.
