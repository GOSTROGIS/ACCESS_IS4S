# Checking a pay stub and filling in a TD1

## Why this matters on the job

On your first day with a new employer in BC, you are usually asked for three things: a TD1 form, a TD1BC form and your banking information for direct deposit. Every payday after that, you get a pay stub. If an hour of overtime is missing or a deduction is wrong, the stub is where you catch it. A few minutes with a calculator can save you money.

## Key ideas

**Gross pay** is what you earned before anything is taken off. **Deductions** are the amounts taken off, such as Canada Pension Plan (CPP), Employment Insurance (EI), income tax and union dues. **Net pay** is what lands in your bank account.

Net pay = gross pay − total deductions

In BC, your employer must give you a wage statement (pay stub) every payday. It must show the employer's name and address, your hours, your wage rate, your overtime hours, each deduction and what it is for, and your gross and net pay.

The **TD1** (federal) and **TD1BC** (British Columbia) forms tell your employer which tax credits you will claim. Your employer uses the total to decide how much income tax to take off each pay. You only have to fill in the TD1BC if your federal total on line 13 is more than $16,452.

For **direct deposit**, your employer needs three numbers from the bottom of a cheque: the branch number (5 digits), the institution number (3 digits) and your account number. The first set of digits on a cheque is the cheque number, which is not needed.

### Worked example

Marie works 40 regular hours at $30.00 an hour and 2 overtime hours at 1.5 times her rate.

- Regular pay: 40 × $30.00 = $1,200.00
- Overtime rate: 1.5 × $30.00 = $45.00 an hour
- Overtime pay: 2 × $45.00 = $90.00
- Gross pay: $1,200.00 + $90.00 = $1,290.00

Her deductions add up to $290.15. Net pay: $1,290.00 − $290.15 = $999.85.

Check: $999.85 + $290.15 = $1,290.00. The check works, so the subtraction is right.
