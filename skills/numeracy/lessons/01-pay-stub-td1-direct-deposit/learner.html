Checking a pay stub and filling in a TD1

Check the numbers on a pay stub, total your claim on the TD1 and TD1BC forms, and read the banking numbers on a cheque for direct deposit.

Why this matters on the job

On your first day with a new employer in BC, you are usually asked for three things: a TD1 form, a TD1BC form and your banking information for direct deposit. Every payday after that, you get a pay stub. If an hour of overtime is missing or a deduction is wrong, the stub is where you catch it. A few minutes with a calculator can save you money.

Key ideas

Gross pay is what you earned before anything is taken off. Deductions are the amounts taken off, such as Canada Pension Plan (CPP), Employment Insurance (EI), income tax and union dues. Net pay is what lands in your bank account.

Net pay = gross pay − total deductions

In BC, your employer must give you a wage statement (pay stub) every payday. It must show the employer's name and address, your hours, your wage rate, your overtime hours, each deduction and what it is for, and your gross and net pay.

The TD1 (federal) and TD1BC (British Columbia) forms tell your employer which tax credits you will claim. Your employer uses the total to decide how much income tax to take off each pay. You only have to fill in the TD1BC if your federal total on line 13 is more than $16,452.

For direct deposit, your employer needs three numbers from the bottom of a cheque: the branch number (5 digits), the institution number (3 digits) and your account number. The first set of digits on a cheque is the cheque number, which is not needed.

Worked example

Marie works 40 regular hours at $30.00 an hour and 2 overtime hours at 1.5 times her rate.

  • Regular pay: 40 × $30.00 = $1,200.00
  • Overtime rate: 1.5 × $30.00 = $45.00 an hour
  • Overtime pay: 2 × $45.00 = $90.00
  • Gross pay: $1,200.00 + $90.00 = $1,290.00

Her deductions add up to $290.15. Net pay: $1,290.00 − $290.15 = $999.85.

Check: $999.85 + $290.15 = $1,290.00. The check works, so the subtraction is right.

On the HP 10bII+

The HP 10bII+ is a financial calculator. Out of the box the HP 10bII+ works in Chain mode: it does each operation in the order you key it, not in BEDMAS order. To see the mode, press RCL, then the blue shift key, then ON (marked Alg/Chain above it). CHAIN or ALGEBRAIC flashes on the screen. The keys below press = after each step, so they give the same answer in either mode.

After ×, the % key divides the number by 100. Marie's pay of $1,290.00:

  • EI: 1290 × 1.63 % = shows 21.03
  • CPP exemption for one pay: 3500 ÷ 26 = shows 134.62. Press →M to keep it in the M memory.
  • CPP bracket: 1290 − RM = shows 1,155.38
  • CPP: × 5.95 % = shows 68.75

The screen rounds to two decimal places, but the calculator keeps 12 digits. →M and RM keep the full exemption, not the rounded 134.62.

Task 1 (level 1). Use Document A. Write down the pay period, the regular hours, the overtime hours, the regular wage rate, the gross pay and the net pay.

Task 2 (level 1). Check Document A against the BC list of what a pay stub must show. Name each item you find. Is anything missing?

Task 3 (level 2). Check the gross pay on Document A. Show the regular pay, the overtime rate, the overtime pay and the total.

Task 4 (level 2). Add the four deductions. Subtract the total from the gross pay. Does your net pay match the stub? Check your subtraction by adding.

Task 5 (level 2). Use Document B to check the EI premium on Document A. Round to the nearest cent.

Task 6 (level 2). Use Document B to check the CPP contribution on Document A. Work out the bracket first: CPP = 5.95% × (gross pay − $3,500 ÷ 26). Inside the bracket, divide before you subtract (BEDMAS). Round to the nearest cent.

Task 7 (level 2). Riley lives alone and has no dependants. In 2026 Riley will pay $1,450 in tuition to a BC technical institute certified by Employment and Social Development Canada. Riley claims nothing else. Fill in the federal TD1 amounts:

  • Line 1, basic personal amount: __
  • Line 5, tuition: __
  • Line 13, total claim amount: __

Does Riley also need to fill in the TD1BC? Explain using the $16,452 rule. If yes, fill in TD1BC line 1 ($13,216), line 4 (tuition, with no Canada Training Credit) and line 11 (total).

Task 8 (level 2). Dana is a new hire at the same company. She has no spouse. Her 7-year-old son lives with her, she supports him, and he has no income. On the TD1, line 8 (amount for an eligible dependant) is the line 1 amount minus the dependant's net income. On the TD1BC, line 7 is $11,317 when the dependant's net income is $1,132 or less. Find Dana's federal line 13 total and her TD1BC line 11 total. She claims nothing else.

Task 9 (level 2). Use Document C. Find the federal claim code for Riley (Task 7) and for Dana (Task 8).

Task 10 (level 1). Use Document D. Write down the branch number, the institution number and the account number. Which set of digits does the employer not need? Name two things you should never give an employer for direct deposit.
