# Is the job on budget?

## Why this matters on the job

An estimate is a promise about money. Once the work starts, someone has to check that promise every week. A crew can be busy all day and still lose money, because the work is costing more per unit than the estimate allowed. The sooner a foreman sees that, the sooner it can be fixed: a better method, a different crew mix, or a talk with the owner about a change. Waiting until the job is done only tells you how much was lost.

This lesson follows [lesson 28, Estimate a job's time and cost](../28-estimate-a-job-time-and-cost/learner.html). It uses the same handrail job.

## Key ideas

### Part A: unit cost

**Estimate.** Estimated cost = estimated quantity × estimated unit rate. For example, 800 m² of drywall at $12.00 a square metre.

**Two percentages that should match.**

- Percent complete = quantity done ÷ total quantity × 100
- Percent spent = cost to date ÷ estimated cost × 100

If percent spent is bigger than percent complete, the work is costing more than planned.

**Actual unit rate.** Actual unit rate = cost to date ÷ quantity done.

**Projected cost at the finish.**

- Quantity left = total quantity − quantity done
- Cost to finish = quantity left × unit rate
- Projected total = cost to date + cost to finish
- Projected over or under = projected total − estimated cost. A plus answer is **over** budget. A minus answer is **under** budget.

**Which rate for the work left?** If the reason for the higher cost is over (a one-time delay, a crew learning a new method), price the rest at the **estimate rate**. If the reason will carry on (the walls are harder than the drawings showed), price the rest at the **actual rate**. When you are not sure, work out both and report the range.

**A wrong quantity.** If the take-off was short, the job loses money even when the crew works at the estimate rate. Always check the quantity as well as the rate.

### Part B: earned value

Earned value compares three numbers at the same date. All three are in dollars.

| Term | Short form | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Budget at completion | BAC | The total budget for the job |
| Planned value | PV | The budget for the work the schedule says should be done by now |
| Earned value | EV | The budget for the work that is **actually** done by now |
| Actual cost | AC | What the work done so far actually cost |

**Variances** (a minus answer is bad news):

- Cost variance: CV = EV − AC. Minus means **over budget**.
- Schedule variance: SV = EV − PV. Minus means **behind schedule**.

**Indexes** (1 is right on plan, less than 1 is bad news):

- Cost performance index: CPI = EV ÷ AC. A CPI of 0.9 means each $1.00 spent earned only $0.90 of work.
- Schedule performance index: SPI = EV ÷ PV. An SPI of 0.8 means the crew has done 80% of the work planned by now.

**Forecast.** If the job keeps costing what it costs now, the estimate at completion is EAC = BAC ÷ CPI. Use the CPI straight from the calculator, not a rounded one. The variance at completion is VAC = BAC − EAC.

### Worked example 1: unit cost on drywall

A drywall crew (fictional) has an estimate of 800 m² of board hung at $12.00 a square metre for labour. Made-up rates for practice.

1. Estimated cost: 800 × $12.00 = **$9,600**.
2. After one week, 300 m² is hung and labour has cost $4,200.
3. Percent complete: 300 ÷ 800 × 100 = **37.5%**. Percent spent: $4,200 ÷ $9,600 × 100 = **43.75%**. More is spent than is done.
4. Actual unit rate: $4,200 ÷ 300 = **$14.00 a square metre**.
5. Quantity left: 800 − 300 = 500 m².
6. At the actual rate: 500 × $14.00 = $7,000. Projected total: $4,200 + $7,000 = $11,200. That is $11,200 − $9,600 = **$1,600 over**.
7. At the estimate rate: 500 × $12.00 = $6,000. Projected total: $4,200 + $6,000 = $10,200. That is **$600 over**.
8. The crew says the slow part is cutting around many small windows, and the rest of the floors are the same. The reason carries on, so **$1,600 over** is the better forecast.
9. Then a field measure shows the walls total 900 m², not 800 m². Quantity left: 900 − 300 = 600 m². At $14.00: 600 × $14.00 = $8,400. Projected total: $4,200 + $8,400 = $12,600. That is **$3,000 over**.

### Worked example 2: earned value on the handrail

Coastline Steel Works (fictional) is building the 10-section handrail from lesson 28. The budget is $700 a section, so BAC = 10 × $700 = $7,000. The schedule says 3 sections a week. At the end of week 2:

- The plan says 6 sections should be done. PV = 6 × $700 = **$4,200**.
- The crew has finished 5 sections. EV = 5 × $700 = **$3,500**.
- Labour and material so far have cost **$4,000**. AC = $4,000.

1. CV = EV − AC = $3,500 − $4,000 = **−$500**. Over budget.
2. SV = EV − PV = $3,500 − $4,200 = **−$700**. Behind schedule, by one section's worth of work.
3. CPI = EV ÷ AC = $3,500 ÷ $4,000 = **0.875**. Each $1.00 spent has earned $0.875 of work.
4. SPI = EV ÷ PV = $3,500 ÷ $4,200 = **0.83**. The crew has done 83% of the work planned by now.
5. EAC = BAC ÷ CPI = $7,000 ÷ 0.875 = **$8,000**. VAC = $7,000 − $8,000 = **−$1,000**.
6. In one sentence: "The handrail is one section behind and $500 over budget, and if nothing changes it will finish about $1,000 over."

The lesson 28 estimate held $700 of contingency, so a $1,000 overrun would use all of it and $300 more.
