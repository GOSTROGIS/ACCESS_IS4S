# Reading a tape measure to the sixteenth

## Why this matters on the job

Every cut a carpenter makes starts with a tape reading. On a framing crew in Langley or Kamloops, a stud, a block or a piece of trim is marked from a reading such as 46 7/16 in. A reading that is off by one sixteenth on every piece adds up along a wall. Plumbers, welders and millwrights read the same inch marks on rules and on bolt, drill and wrench sizes.

## Key ideas

**One inch is split into equal spaces.** On most tapes the longest lines mark whole inches. The next longest line marks the half inch, then quarters, then eighths. The shortest lines mark sixteenths, so there are 16 equal spaces in one inch.

**The denominator names the size of the space.** In 7/16, the 16 says the inch is split into 16 equal spaces. The 7 says how many of those spaces you counted.

**Reading a mark, step by step:**

1. Find the last whole inch line before the mark. That is the whole number.
2. Count the sixteenth spaces from that inch line to the mark.
3. Write the count over 16.
4. Put the fraction in lowest terms.
5. Say the whole reading out loud, whole inches and fraction, so a partner can check it.

**Count spaces, not lines.** Start counting at the first space after the inch line. If you count the inch line itself as 1, every reading comes out 1/16 in too long.

**Lowest terms.** Divide the top and the bottom by the same number until no number bigger than 1 goes into both. A quick test for tape fractions: if the numerator is odd, the fraction is already in lowest terms. If it is even, divide the top and the bottom by 2 and test again. Lowest terms change the name of the mark, not where it is: 8/16, 4/8, 2/4 and 1/2 all name the same line.

**Comparing lengths.** Write both fractions over the same denominator first. For tape fractions, 16 works for halves, quarters, eighths and sixteenths. Then the bigger numerator is the longer length.

**Let the tool set the precision.** A tape marked in sixteenths gives readings to the nearest 1/16 in. Do not write a finer reading than the marks support.

### Worked example

A mark is ten sixteenth-inch spaces past the 2 in line.

- Whole number: 2
- Fraction: 10/16. 10 is even, so divide the top and the bottom by 2: 5/8.
- Reading: 2 5/8 in
- Check: the mark sits between the 2 1/2 in line and the 2 3/4 in line. 2 5/8 is between them, so the reading makes sense. Writing only "5/8" would lose the two whole inches.

a) A mark six sixteenth-inch spaces from zero is recorded as 6/6 in.
b) A mark ten sixteenth-inch spaces past the 2 in line is recorded as 5/8 in.
c) A learner writes 12/16 in lowest terms as 3/16.
d) A mark is six sixteenth-inch spaces past the 5 in line. A learner counted the 5 in line as the first space and recorded 5 7/16 in.

**Task 5** (level 2). Compare the sizes. Show the common denominator you used.

a) Which is longer, 11/16 in or 3/4 in? By how much?

c) A socket set is marked in sixteenths. What size comes right after the 1/2 in socket?
d) Which is bigger, a 9/16 in socket or a 5/8 in socket? By how much?
e) Which is longer, 1 5/8 in or 1 9/16 in? By how much?
